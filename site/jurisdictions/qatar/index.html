      State of Qatar

      Qatar: Investigative Consulting

      Investigative consulting for matters touching Qatar works across two legal environments: the civil law system administered by the State's courts and the common law-based Qatar Financial Centre. Engagements centre on counterparty and ownership research, contract dispute support and careful handling of personal data under Law No. 13 of 2016.

      Overview

      Qatar at a Glance

      Qatar's legal system combines civil law and Islamic Sharia, with the Constitution at its apex and commercial relationships governed largely by the Civil Code (Law No. 22 of 2004) and the Commercial Companies Law (Law No. 11 of 2015, as amended). Onshore companies register with the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, while the Qatar Financial Centre operates a separate regime modelled on English common law, with its own court and regulatory tribunal. Foreign ownership rules under Law No. 1 of 2019 mean many businesses involve local partners or approved structures. Investigators must recognise which regime governs an entity, accept that Arabic is the principal language of official records, and respect a legal culture in which privacy and reputation carry significant weight.

      Organisations commission work involving Qatar chiefly because of its position as one of the world's largest exporters of liquefied natural gas, with QatarEnergy, the state-owned operator, pursuing a major expansion of production toward 2030. That programme, together with infrastructure, real estate, logistics, hospitality and sovereign investment activity, generates contracts with international suppliers, subcontractors and joint venture partners. Typical exposures include opaque ownership behind local partners and agents, subcontractor integrity in large projects, payment and contract disputes, procurement irregularities and reputational questions about intermediaries. International investors and lenders also seek background research on Qatari counterparties before transactions abroad.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Qatar

      Legal system

      Qatar has a civil law system incorporating Islamic Sharia, with the Constitution ranking highest. Contracts are governed principally by the Civil Code (Law No. 22 of 2004). Ordinary commercial disputes proceed through the courts of first instance, the Court of Appeal and the Court of Cassation; Law No. 21 of 2021 provides for a dedicated Investment and Commerce Court. The Qatar Financial Centre has its own common law-based Civil and Commercial Court and Regulatory Tribunal, administered by the Qatar International Court and Dispute Resolution Centre, which also offers arbitration and mediation services.

      Data protection

      Law No. (13) of 2016 Concerning Personal Data Protection · Regulator: National Cyber Governance and Assurance Affairs (NCGAA), National Cyber Security Agency

      Qatar was the first GCC state to adopt a generally applicable data protection law. Processing generally requires consent, subject to exceptions including legal obligations, the public interest and criminal investigations, and data subjects must be told the controller's identity and purpose before processing. Sensitive personal data requires regulator authorisation and an impact assessment. Breaches likely to cause damage must be notified within 72 hours. Cross-border transfers are permitted where they serve a lawful purpose and comply with the law. The Qatar Financial Centre applies its own data protection regulations to firms within it, so the applicable regime must be identified at the outset.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified, from official sources available to us, a published licensing regime for private investigators in Qatar comparable to those in many other jurisdictions. Security services are regulated through the Ministry of Interior, and any investigative activity on the ground would need to be assessed for licensing and lawfulness with Qatari counsel before it begins. Surveillance, photography of individuals, recording and attempts to obtain personal information carry legal and reputational risk in Qatar. TDSC therefore treats field activity as exceptional and scopes engagements around documentary research and lawful interviews coordinated through appropriately qualified local professionals.

      Corporate & public records

      Onshore companies are registered in the commercial registry maintained by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry under the Commercial Companies Law. Entities licensed in the Qatar Financial Centre, the Qatar Free Zones Authority and Qatar Science and Technology Park are registered under their own rules, and the QFC maintains a public register of firms. Public access to shareholder, officer and beneficial ownership details for onshore companies is limited and generally does not match the depth of European registries. Court judgments of the QFC Civil and Commercial Court are published; access to onshore court files is generally restricted to parties. Corporate research usually combines registry extracts obtained through lawful channels with media, judgment and trade sources.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery is criminalised under the Penal Code (Law No. 11 of 2004), with penalties of up to ten years' imprisonment, and Law No. 22 of 2015 addresses corrupt officials. The Administrative Control and Transparency Authority, restructured by Decree No. 6 of 2015, is the principal anti-corruption body. Anti-money laundering and counter-terrorist financing obligations are set by Law No. 20 of 2019, and Law No. 4 of 2022 restricts cash transactions above QAR 50,000. Qatar ratified the UN Convention against Corruption in 2007.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Qatar

      TDSC scopes Qatar engagements from Nashville and first determines whether the entities involved sit onshore, in the Qatar Financial Centre or in a free zone, because registries, courts and data protection rules differ between them. Research relies on lawfully available registry information, published judgments, regulatory disclosures, trade sources and Arabic and English media, reviewed by analysts able to work with both. Given the absence of an identified licensing regime for private investigators and the sensitivity of surveillance and recording, TDSC does not propose covert fieldwork in Qatar; any local inquiries are planned with Qatari counsel and conducted through appropriately qualified local professionals. Personal data is minimised and handled in line with Law No. 13 of 2016 and the client's own obligations.

      TDSC has no office in Qatar. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Qatar: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can TDSC confirm who ultimately owns a Qatari company?

      Ownership research in Qatar is often harder than in Europe because public access to shareholder and beneficial ownership details for onshore companies is limited. We work from lawfully obtained registry information, QFC records where relevant, judgments, regulatory disclosures and media, and we state clearly where the evidence supports a conclusion and where ownership remains unconfirmed.

      Does it matter whether a company is registered in the Qatar Financial Centre?

      Yes. QFC entities are governed by a separate regime based on English common law, with their own register, court and data protection rules. Onshore companies fall under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry and the State courts. Identifying the correct regime early determines which records exist and how disputes are likely to be resolved.

      Is surveillance an option in Qatar?

      We do not propose covert surveillance in Qatar. Photography and recording of individuals and intrusions into private life carry legal and reputational risk, and we have not identified a licensing framework that would support such work by private investigators. Where facts must be established locally, we plan lawful alternatives with Qatari counsel.

      How does Qatar's data protection law affect an internal investigation?

      Law No. 13 of 2016 generally requires a lawful basis and notice for processing personal data, with stricter rules for sensitive data and a 72-hour breach notification duty. Employee data collected during an investigation should be limited to what the inquiry needs, and transfers out of Qatar should be documented as serving a lawful purpose.

      Can you support arbitration involving a Qatari party?

      Yes, from outside Qatar. We assist counsel with background research on parties and witnesses, corporate history, related entities and lawful asset research relevant to enforcement. Arabic-language records are reviewed directly, and we coordinate with Qatari counsel on any step that requires local action or court process.
