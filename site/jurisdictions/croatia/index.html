      Overview

      Croatia at a Glance

      Croatia is an EU member state whose legal system has been closely harmonised with EU law. It adopted the euro in 2023 and joined the Schengen Area the same year. For investigators this means a familiar European framework: the GDPR applies, supplemented by a national implementing act, and core registries are open. The court register of companies kept by the commercial courts can be consulted online free of charge, the land registers kept by the municipal courts are public, and basic beneficial ownership data is available to citizens through the e-Građani system. Local practice, language and the role of the courts still shape how information is gathered.

      Organisations commission work involving Croatia because of its tourism economy, which accounts for a significant share of GDP, its Adriatic real estate market, and its position as a trading partner of Germany, Italy, Slovenia and the Western Balkans. Typical exposures include acquisitions of hotels and coastal property, energy and infrastructure projects, shipbuilding and port businesses, and distribution arrangements reaching into Bosnia and Herzegovina and Serbia. Questions arise over hidden ownership, related-party dealings, disputed property rights with a long registration history, and the integrity of counterparties involved in public procurement and EU-funded projects.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Croatia

      Legal system

      Croatia has a civil law system, historically influenced by Austrian law, with core legislation including the Civil Obligations Act and the Companies Act. Commercial disputes go to nine specialised commercial courts (trgovački sudovi), with appeals to the High Commercial Court, which has national jurisdiction; the Supreme Court is the highest court of appeal and cassation, and the Constitutional Court protects constitutional rights. Commercial arbitration is commonly administered by the Permanent Arbitration Court at the Croatian Chamber of Economy (Stalno arbitražno sudište pri Hrvatskoj gospodarskoj komori) in Zagreb.

      Data protection

      General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and the Act on the Implementation of the General Data Protection Regulation (2018) · Regulator: Agencija za zaštitu osobnih podataka (AZOP), the Croatian Personal Data Protection Agency

      Investigative work involving personal data needs a GDPR lawful basis, typically legitimate interests or the establishment and defence of legal claims, supported by a documented balancing test and data minimisation. The Croatian implementing act adds specific rules: workplace video surveillance requires prior notice to all employees and is excluded from rest and changing areas, recordings may generally be kept for six months, and biometric time-keeping is restricted. Transfers of findings outside the EEA follow GDPR Chapter V, through adequacy, standard contractual clauses or a narrow derogation; Croatia adds no extra transfer requirements. Decisions of AZOP can be challenged before the administrative courts.

      Private investigators

      Private detective activity in Croatia is a regulated profession governed by dedicated national legislation, with oversight connected to the Ministry of the Interior; private security services are regulated separately. We were not able to open the current consolidated text of the detective legislation during this research, so the precise licensing conditions, permitted methods and the role of any professional chamber are not summarised here. In practice, any enquiries carried out in Croatia are delegated to locally authorised detectives and reviewed with Croatian counsel, and methods are assessed against the GDPR, the implementing act's surveillance rules and criminal law protections for privacy and communications.

      Corporate & public records

      The court register (sudski registar) is kept by the commercial courts and can be consulted online free of charge without showing a legitimate interest; it shows company details, registered capital, management and supervisory board members and, for limited liability companies, members, and supporting documents can be examined. Financial statements are filed with FINA. The Register of Beneficial Owners is operated by FINA on behalf of the Ministry of Finance's Anti-Money Laundering Office; citizens can view basic data free through e-Građani, including name, month and year of birth, residence, citizenship and the nature and extent of ownership. Land registers kept by the municipal courts are public.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery offences are set out in the Criminal Code, and legal entities can be held criminally liable under a separate act on the liability of legal persons for criminal offences. Money laundering prevention is governed by the Act on the Prevention of Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing, administered by the Anti-Money Laundering Office of the Ministry of Finance, which also oversees the beneficial ownership register. Corruption and organised crime are prosecuted by USKOK, the Office for the Suppression of Corruption and Organised Crime, established in 2001 within the State Attorney's Office.

      Questions

      Croatia: Frequently Asked Questions

      Is the Croatian company register publicly accessible?

      Yes. The court register kept by the commercial courts can be searched online free of charge, and the general book and supporting documents can be examined without showing a legitimate interest, subject to statutory exceptions. It shows company details, capital and management, which makes it the starting point for most corporate due diligence involving Croatian entities.

      Can beneficial ownership be checked in Croatia?

      To a degree. The Register of Beneficial Owners is operated by FINA for the Anti-Money Laundering Office. Basic data, including name, month and year of birth, country of residence, citizenship and the nature and extent of ownership, can be viewed free through e-Građani. Fuller access is reserved to obligated entities for their own customers.

      Are land records reliable for coastal property?

      The land registers kept by the municipal courts are public and combine cadastral data with ownership and other rights. For coastal and island property, records can reflect long and sometimes unresolved histories involving restitution, inheritance and maritime domain rules, so registry review is paired with litigation searches and the advice of Croatian counsel.

      What does GDPR mean for an investigation in Croatia?

      Personal data must be collected under a lawful basis, usually legitimate interests or legal claims, with a documented balancing test and minimisation. The Croatian implementing act adds rules on video surveillance and biometric data. Transfers of results outside the EEA require GDPR Chapter V safeguards. AZOP is the supervisory authority.

      Who investigates corruption cases in Croatia?

      USKOK, the Office for the Suppression of Corruption and Organised Crime, operates within the State Attorney's Office and handles serious corruption and organised crime. Its indictments and related court proceedings are often relevant background in integrity due diligence on Croatian companies and individuals connected to public procurement or state-owned enterprises.
