      Overview

      Mozambique at a Glance

      Mozambique is a Portuguese-speaking civil-law jurisdiction whose commercial framework derives largely from Portuguese-Roman law. Company information sits with the Legal Entities Registry (CREL), which since March 2024 has also held beneficial ownership declarations under Decree-Law no. 1/2024. Litigation is generally slow, and commercial parties frequently turn to arbitration under Law no. 11/99. As of late 2026 the country still has no comprehensive personal data protection statute; a draft bill remains under preparation, and the Electronic Transactions Act and constitutional privacy guarantees govern much of the current landscape. Investigators therefore work with a mix of public registries, published notices in the Boletim da República and careful field enquiry.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving Mozambique mainly because of the scale of capital entering the extractive sector and the governance risks that accompany it. The Mozambique LNG project in Cabo Delgado announced a full restart of onshore and offshore activity in January 2026 after years of force majeure, drawing contractors, subcontractors and local-content partners into the province. Coal, graphite and heavy-sands projects, the Beira and Nacala logistics corridors, and donor-funded infrastructure create further demand for counterparty diligence. The hidden-debts affair and subsequent prosecutions have left lenders and investors attentive to politically exposed persons, procurement integrity and the true ownership of local partners.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Mozambique

      Legal system

      Mozambique has a civil-law system based largely on Portuguese-Roman law, with codified civil, commercial and criminal law; the current Penal Code is Law no. 24/2019. Commercial claims are heard in the judicial courts, with larger claims typically starting at provincial level, and litigation commonly takes years. Arbitration is governed by the Law on Arbitration, Conciliation and Mediation (Law no. 11/99), which draws on the UNCITRAL Model Law. The principal institution is the Centre for Arbitration, Conciliation and Mediation (CACM) in Maputo, with a delegation in Beira.

      Data protection

      No comprehensive data protection law as of November 2026; Electronic Transactions Act (Law no. 3/2017) and constitutional privacy provisions apply · Regulator: National Institute for Information and Communication Technologies (INTIC) for electronic transactions; no dedicated data protection authority yet

      Mozambique has not yet enacted a standalone personal data protection law; a draft bill was still being prepared for submission to parliament in August 2026. In the meantime the Electronic Transactions Act imposes duties on controllers and processors of electronic personal data, including the appointment of a data protection officer, and INTIC has begun inspections. Constitutional privacy rights and employment-law protections also limit intrusive collection, particularly concerning employees. Investigative work should be designed to the standard of the client's home regime, with data minimisation and documented purpose, and revisited once the new law is adopted.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified a dedicated statute licensing private investigators or detective agencies in Mozambique. Private security companies are licensed and supervised by the Ministry of the Interior under Decree no. 9/2007, and civil-society research in 2025 described that oversight as limited. Whether a particular investigative activity falls within the private security framework, or requires other authorisation, should be confirmed with Mozambican counsel before fieldwork. Covert recording, interception of communications and access to non-public state records are not available routes, and interviews and site visits in sensitive provinces such as Cabo Delgado carry security considerations of their own.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies are registered with the Legal Entities Registry (Conservatória do Registo das Entidades Legais, CREL). Registration certificates showing constitution, directors and statutory changes can be obtained, and incorporations and amendments are published in the Boletim da República. Under Decree-Law no. 1/2024 companies, consortia and representations of foreign entities must declare beneficial owners to CREL; supervisory and enforcement bodies have access, and interested third parties may apply for access. Filing of annual accounts is required for certain entities, but coverage is uneven. Court and land records are not consistently available online and usually require local retrieval.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery in the public and private sectors is criminalised under the Penal Code (Law no. 24/2019). Law no. 6/2004 established the Central Office for the Fight Against Corruption (GCCC), a specialised unit within the Attorney General's Office (PGR). Anti-money laundering obligations are set out in Law no. 14/2023 of 28 August 2023, which repealed Law no. 11/2022; the Financial Intelligence Office of Mozambique (GIFiM) is the financial intelligence unit and supervises reporting entities alongside sector regulators.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Mozambique

      TDSC scopes Mozambique matters from Nashville, beginning with a jurisdictional review of what the engagement requires: registry work, field enquiry, interviews or asset research. Because Mozambique has no dedicated investigator licensing regime that we have identified and no comprehensive data protection law yet, we confirm the permissible scope of any local activity with Mozambican counsel and apply data-handling standards drawn from the client's own regulatory regime. Registry retrieval from CREL and review of the Boletim da República are carried out through qualified local professionals. Where work touches Cabo Delgado or other insecure areas, we assess whether field enquiry is proportionate at all. Findings are sourced, dated and suitable for counsel's review.

      TDSC has no office in Mozambique. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Mozambique: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can the true owners of a Mozambican company be identified?

      Often, yes, though not always from a single source. Since Decree-Law no. 1/2024, companies must declare beneficial owners to CREL, and interested third parties may apply for access. Declarations are self-reported, so we compare them with Boletim da República notices, foreign holding-company filings and press records to test whether the declared owner is the real controller.

      Is there a data protection authority in Mozambique?

      Not a dedicated one as of November 2026. A draft personal data protection law was still being prepared for parliament in August 2026. INTIC oversees obligations under the Electronic Transactions Act, including the appointment of data protection officers. We plan engagements so they would remain defensible once the new law is in force.

      How reliable are court records for litigation searches?

      Court records are largely paper-based and not centrally searchable online. Litigation searches usually require retrieval at specific provincial or district courts by local professionals, and coverage depends on knowing where proceedings were likely filed. We state the courts searched and the limits of any negative result.

      Can you conduct enquiries in Cabo Delgado?

      Sometimes, but only after a specific risk assessment. Security conditions in parts of the province have restricted movement for years, and the safety of local sources and professionals comes first. Many questions about Cabo Delgado counterparties can be answered through registry work, project documents and interviews conducted outside the province.

      Do documents for Mozambican proceedings need translation?

      Court proceedings and registry filings are conducted in Portuguese. Foreign documents generally require legalisation and certified Portuguese translation before they are accepted, and counsel should confirm the exact requirements for the specific court or registry. We can prepare reports with Portuguese-language source extracts preserved for that purpose.
