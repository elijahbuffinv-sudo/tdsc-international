      Japan: Investigative Consulting

      Investigative work involving Japan combines a well-organised public registry system with firm expectations of privacy and process.       Overview

      Japan at a Glance

      Japan is a civil law jurisdiction with a stable, document-driven business environment. Commercial and real estate registers kept by the Legal Affairs Bureaus are among the more accessible in Asia, and the Act on the Protection of Personal Information, substantially amended with effect from 1 April 2022, sets clear rules on personal and sensitive information. Private investigation is a lawful, regulated business: the 2006 detective business act requires notification to the prefectural public safety commission and places conduct obligations on investigators. Relationships, reputation and consensus weigh heavily in Japanese business, and matters are often resolved quietly, so findings must be accurate, well documented and handled with discretion.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving Japan for acquisitions and joint ventures, distributor and supplier relationships, and disputes with Japanese counterparties. Japan is a major source of outbound investment, so many matters concern Japanese parent companies and their overseas subsidiaries, where governance gaps, accounting irregularities or improper payments surface far from Tokyo. Other recurring needs include screening for links to organised crime groups before transactions, trade secret leakage involving departing staff, verification of executives and partners, and locating assets for enforcement. Language, the structure of corporate groups and conservative disclosure practices mean that public information is reliable but rarely self-explanatory.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Japan Concentrates

      01

      Acquisition and joint venture diligence

      Reviewing Japanese targets and partners through commercial and real estate registers, litigation and insolvency notices, Japanese-language media and industry sources, to test management backgrounds, related companies and undisclosed liabilities before signing, and to frame questions for Japanese counsel and the deal team.

      02

      Antisocial forces screening

      Supporting counterparty screening for links to organised crime groups and other antisocial forces, a standard element of Japanese contracting and banking practice, using lawful public and media sources and coordinating with specialist local providers and counsel where deeper inquiry is justified.

      03

      Overseas subsidiary misconduct

      Assisting Japanese parent companies, and their counsel, investigating accounting irregularities, improper payments or conflicts of interest at subsidiaries in the United States and elsewhere, including fact development, interviews arranged through counsel, and bilingual reporting suited to Japanese board and audit committee review.

      04

      Trade secret and employee departures

      Supporting counsel where confidential technical or customer information may have left with departing employees or partners, mapping new affiliations and corporate links through open sources, with any local inquiries handled by notified Japanese investigators and evidence preserved for proceedings under the Unfair Competition Prevention Act.

      05

      Asset tracing for enforcement

      Identifying real estate holdings and corporate interests of a Japanese debtor through registry records, published filings and open sources, to support enforcement of judgments or arbitral awards led by Japanese counsel, while respecting APPI limits on the handling of personal information.

      Law & Regulation

      What applies before any work touching Japan begins

      Legal system

      Japan has a civil law system based on codes, including the Civil Code, Commercial Code, Companies Act and Code of Civil Procedure, drawing historically on German and French models with post-war American influence in public and corporate law. Commercial disputes are heard by district courts, with appeals to the high courts and the Supreme Court; the Tokyo and Osaka District Courts have divisions focused on commercial and insolvency matters, and the Intellectual Property High Court handles patent appeals. The Japan Commercial Arbitration Association (JCAA) administers commercial arbitration and mediation under rules most recently amended in 2021.

      Data protection

      Act on the Protection of Personal Information (APPI, 2003; amended with effect from 1 April 2022) · Regulator: Personal Information Protection Commission (PPC)

      The APPI requires businesses to specify the purpose of use, notify or publish it, and process personal information only within that purpose. Sensitive personal information, which includes medical history, criminal records and history of being a crime victim, generally requires consent to obtain and cannot be shared by opt-out. Transfers to third parties abroad require prior consent naming the destination country and information about its protection regime, unless the recipient is in a country recognised by the PPC, such as the UK and EU states, or maintains equivalent safeguards. Investigative reports containing personal data must be handled within these limits. A triennial review policy was decided in January 2026, so further amendments are expected.

      Private investigators

      Private investigation in Japan is regulated by the Act on the Proper Operation of Detective Business (Act No. 60 of 2006). Anyone conducting investigations into a person's whereabouts or activities for clients through interviews, tailing or surveillance must notify the prefectural public safety commission, and certain persons are disqualified. Investigators must act within the law without infringing others' rights or peaceful lives, must refuse work they know will be used for crime or unlawful discrimination, must give clients written explanations and contracts, and must keep secrets confidential. Authorities can require reports, inspect and suspend businesses. Investigators have only the powers of ordinary citizens and no special access to official records.

      Corporate & public records

      Commercial registration is maintained by the Legal Affairs Bureaus under the Ministry of Justice. Registry extracts showing a company's name, purpose, capital, representative directors, directors and auditors, and registered address can be obtained by anyone, including online through the paid Registry Information Service operated by the Minji Homu Kyokai foundation, which also provides real estate registry records and cadastral maps. Shareholder registers are not publicly filed. Since 31 January 2022 companies may voluntarily lodge a beneficial owner list with the registry; certified copies are issued on the company's application for use with banks, and the list is not a public register.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery of public officials is a crime under the Penal Code, and bribery of foreign public officials is an offence under the Unfair Competition Prevention Act, supported by Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry guidelines revised in February 2024. Prosecutions are brought by the Public Prosecutors Office, which has special investigation departments in major cities. Anti-money laundering obligations arise principally under the Act on Prevention of Transfer of Criminal Proceeds, with the National Police Agency's financial intelligence unit and the Financial Services Agency as key authorities.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Japan

      TDSC begins each Japan matter by confirming the purpose and lawful basis for the inquiry, including whether personal or sensitive information under the APPI will be collected and whether results will leave Japan. Research from Nashville draws on registry extracts, real estate records, court and insolvency notices, Japanese-language media and corporate disclosures, reviewed by Japanese-reading staff or translators. Where interviews, site visits or surveillance are justified, they are carried out by investigators notified to the relevant prefectural public safety commission, under written terms that meet the detective business act. TDSC does not seek non-public government, telecom or banking records. Legal questions, including evidence use in Japanese proceedings, are referred to Japanese counsel, and reports are written for bilingual use.

      TDSC has no office in Japan. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Japan: Frequently Asked Questions

      Are private investigators licensed in Japan?

      Japan uses a notification system rather than a licence. Under the 2006 detective business act, investigators who conduct interviews, tailing or surveillance for clients must notify the prefectural public safety commission, observe conduct rules and provide written contracts. When local fieldwork is needed, TDSC coordinates only with investigators who have completed that notification and work within its limits.

      What can be learned about a Japanese company from public records?

      Commercial registry extracts show the company's purpose, capital, registered address, representative directors, directors and auditors, and are available to anyone, including online. Real estate registers show ownership and mortgages. Shareholder registers are not publicly filed, so ownership of private companies is usually reconstructed from disclosures, group filings, media and industry sources.

      Is there a beneficial ownership register in Japan?

      Since 31 January 2022 companies may voluntarily lodge a list of beneficial owners with the Legal Affairs Bureau, and the registry issues certified copies on the company's application, mainly for bank due diligence. It is not a public register, so third parties cannot search it. Ownership questions are approached through other lawful sources.

      Can investigation findings about Japanese individuals be sent to the United States?

      Possibly, but the APPI governs transfers of personal data abroad. Absent an exception, a Japanese business transferring data to a recipient in the United States generally needs consent naming the country, or the recipient must maintain equivalent safeguards. TDSC plans data flows with the client and counsel at the outset and limits personal information to what the matter requires.

      Does TDSC assist with investigations of overseas subsidiaries of Japanese companies?

      Yes. This is a common need: misconduct at a foreign subsidiary may be detected late by a Tokyo head office. TDSC supports counsel and audit committees with fact development, background research and structured reporting that can be translated for Japanese management, while legal advice on Japanese obligations remains with Japanese counsel.
