      Republic of Colombia

      Investigative Consulting Involving Colombia

      Matters touching Colombia involve a civil law system, a commercial registry run by regional chambers of commerce, an active data protection regulator and a well-developed corporate anti-corruption regime. Investigative consulting here means rigorous counterparty research, careful handling of personal data and close coordination with Colombian counsel and authorised professionals.

      Overview

      Colombia at a Glance

      Colombia has about 52.9 million people (World Bank, 2024) and a civil law system under the 1991 Constitution. The commercial registry is kept by regional chambers of commerce, whose records are aggregated in the RUES national business register. Personal data is governed by Law 1581 of 2012, enforced by the Superintendence of Industry and Commerce (SIC), with stricter rules on financial and credit data under Law 1266 of 2008. Companies face administrative liability for transnational bribery under Law 1778 of 2016 and, since Law 2195 of 2022, for a wider range of corruption offences committed by their managers. Access to the beneficial ownership registry kept by the tax authority is restricted to designated public bodies.

      Organisations commission work involving Colombia because of its oil, coal, gold and emerald production, coffee, flower and fruit exports, infrastructure concessions, and its growing role as a regional base for services and technology. Typical exposures include counterparty and acquisition due diligence, partners who deal with public entities and state contracts, supply chain integrity in sectors exposed to illegal mining or narcotics-related finance, asset tracing in support of judgments and arbitral awards, and internal fraud by distributors or managers. The presence of armed and criminal groups in some regions makes reputational research and the physical safety of any fieldwork important planning considerations.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Colombia Concentrates

      01

      Counterparty and acquisition due diligence

      Research on Colombian targets, partners and their managers using chamber of commerce certificates, court records, sanctions and disciplinary databases, and media, with attention to public contracting history, transnational bribery exposure and links to persons of concern before a transaction or partnership proceeds.

      02

      Extractive sector supply chains

      Review of gold, coal, oil and emerald producers, traders and contractors, where buyers and lenders need to understand ownership, licensing relationships and any links to illegal mining or armed groups in the regions where production takes place.

      03

      Public contracts and concessions

      Integrity research on partners in infrastructure concessions and state contracts, including prior sanctions, fiscal liability findings and contracting bans, since Law 2195 of 2022 exposes companies to administrative liability for corruption committed by their managers.

      04

      Asset tracing for enforcement

      Lawful research into real estate, company interests, vehicles and litigation of debtors with Colombian connections, supporting Colombian counsel enforcing foreign judgments, arbitral awards or creditor claims in insolvency proceedings before Supersociedades or the ordinary courts.

      05

      Agricultural export integrity

      Background research on coffee, flower, banana and avocado exporters and intermediaries for buyers, traders and financiers, covering ownership, litigation, reputational issues and the risk that legitimate export channels are being used to move illicit proceeds abroad.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Colombia

      Legal system

      Colombia has a civil law system based on the 1991 Constitution, the Civil Code and the Commercial Code. Ordinary civil and commercial disputes go through circuit courts and superior district tribunals, with the Supreme Court of Justice at the top. The Council of State hears administrative disputes and the Constitutional Court decides constitutional matters. The Superintendence of Companies (Supersociedades) exercises judicial functions in certain corporate disputes and handles insolvency proceedings. Arbitration is widely used in commercial and state contracts, and the Arbitration and Conciliation Center of the Bogotá Chamber of Commerce is the leading institution.

      Data protection

      Statutory Law 1581 of 2012, Decree 1377 of 2013 and Law 1266 of 2008 (financial and credit data) · Regulator: Superintendence of Industry and Commerce (SIC)

      Law 1581 requires prior, informed authorisation from the data subject for most processing of private data, and silence does not count as consent. Sensitive data, including health, political views and biometrics, needs explicit consent unless an exception applies. Larger controllers must register their databases in the National Register of Databases (RNBD). Transfers abroad are prohibited unless the destination offers adequate protection or an exception applies, such as express consent; the SIC recommends the Ibero-American model contractual clauses. Transmissions to processors need a written agreement. Unauthorised handling of personal data for gain is also a criminal offence.

      Private investigators

      Colombia has no standalone statute licensing private detectives that we could identify. Decree-Law 356 of 1994, the private security statute supervised by the Superintendence of Private Surveillance and Security, lists security advisory, consultancy and investigation services within its scope and allows surveillance companies and specialised cooperatives to offer them as related services. The decree does not clearly set out a separate licence for these services. Activities remain constrained by Law 1581 on personal data, the Criminal Code provisions on unlawful interception and abusive access to information systems, and constitutional privacy protections. Bank and tax records are confidential, and fieldwork is scoped with Colombian counsel.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies register with the chamber of commerce of their domicile, which keeps the commercial registry and issues certificates of existence and legal representation showing legal name, corporate purpose, legal representatives, directors and registered acts. The RUES portal, associated with Confecámaras, allows searches across chambers. Shareholders of simplified joint-stock companies (SAS) and corporations are generally recorded in share registry books rather than public filings. Under Law 2195 of 2022, access to the Registro Único de Beneficiarios Finales is limited to listed authorities such as DIAN, the Attorney General's Office and Supersociedades, so it is not public.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Law 1778 of 2016 imposes administrative liability on companies for bribery of foreign public officials, investigated and sanctioned by Supersociedades, with fines of up to 200,000 monthly minimum wages and contracting bans of up to 20 years. Law 2195 of 2022 extended corporate liability to domestic corruption offences, required transparency and business ethics programmes, and restricted beneficial ownership registry access. The Attorney General's Office (Fiscalía), the Inspector General (Procuraduría) and the Comptroller General (Contraloría) are key enforcement bodies.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Colombia

      TDSC scopes Colombian matters from Nashville, starting with which chambers of commerce, courts and authorities hold relevant records and whether the matter touches public contracting or sanctioned conduct. Before research begins we define the purpose of any personal data processing under Law 1581, minimise sensitive data and plan a lawful route for transferring results abroad. Records research draws on chamber of commerce certificates, RUES, court systems, official sanctions and disciplinary records and open sources. Where fieldwork, interviews or document retrieval in Colombia are needed, we coordinate appropriately authorised local professionals, assess security conditions in the relevant region and work alongside Colombian counsel. We do not seek access to the beneficial ownership registry, bank or tax records.

      TDSC has no office in Colombia. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Colombia: Frequently Asked Questions

      How do we confirm who represents a Colombian company?

      The chamber of commerce of the company's domicile issues a certificate of existence and legal representation showing the legal name, purpose, legal representatives, directors and registered acts, including any limits on a representative's powers. The certificate should be recent when relied on for contracting. The RUES portal helps identify the competent chamber and other registrations.

      Can we find the beneficial owners of a Colombian company?

      Not from the beneficial ownership registry itself. Law 2195 of 2022 limits access to the Registro Único de Beneficiarios Finales to listed authorities. Ownership is instead reconstructed from chamber of commerce records, share registry information supplied by the counterparty, filings of supervised companies, court records and open sources, and gaps are reported clearly.

      Are private investigators licensed in Colombia?

      We have not identified a dedicated detective licensing law. Decree-Law 356 of 1994 brings security advisory, consultancy and investigation services within the private security statute supervised by the Superintendence of Private Surveillance and Security, but its licensing provisions are not clearly drafted for these services. TDSC confirms requirements with Colombian counsel and works only with appropriately authorised providers.

      What does Law 1581 mean for a background check on a Colombian national?

      Most processing of private data needs the person's prior, informed authorisation, so research without consent must fit an exception or rely on public data, and must be proportionate to its purpose. Sensitive data such as health or political views needs particular care. Transfers to the United States need an adequate-protection basis or an exception such as express consent.

      Why do acquirers focus on transnational bribery in Colombia?

      Law 1778 of 2016 makes Colombian-domiciled companies and their parents or subsidiaries administratively liable for bribing foreign officials, with large fines and contracting bans. Supersociedades investigates and can reward cooperation. Acquirers therefore review a target's ethics programme, prior investigations and international business dealings, and TDSC supplies the factual research behind that assessment.
