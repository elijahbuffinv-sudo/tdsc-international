      Arab Republic of Egypt

      Investigative Consulting Involving Egypt

      In Egypt, investigative consulting has to account for a large economic role played by state and military entities, a licensing-based data protection law that restricts cross-border transfers, and security sensitivities that limit what can be done on the ground. The work concentrates on counterparties, ownership and documented litigation.

      Overview

      Egypt at a Glance

      Egypt is the most populous Arab country, with about 118 million people. Its civil law system is modelled on the French Civil Code, and personal status matters are governed by religious law. Commercial disputes go to the economic courts or to arbitration under Law 27 of 1994. Appeals can take years, and investors describe enforcement as inconsistent. State-owned and military-owned enterprises play a large part in the economy, and companies must obtain a security clearance as well as their commercial and tax registrations. Personal Data Protection Law No. 151 of 2020 requires licences for many processing activities and for transfers abroad, which has a direct effect on how investigative research can be carried out and reported.

      Organisations commission work involving Egypt because of its weight in energy, logistics and construction. Natural gas has become a central sector since the Zohr discovery in 2015. The Suez Canal Economic Zone hosts manufacturing in pharmaceuticals, automotive, textiles and petrochemicals. Remittances, tourism and large real estate and infrastructure projects bring in further capital. Typical needs include vetting joint venture and distribution partners, establishing ownership and links to state or military entities, checking exposure to politically exposed persons, assessing contractors on large projects, and tracing the assets of counterparties after the currency devaluations that preceded the move to a flexible exchange rate in March 2024.

      Jurisdictional Framework

      The rules that govern lawful inquiry in Egypt

      Legal system

      Egypt has a civil law system based mainly on the French Civil Code, with Islamic law influences. Personal status matters follow the religious law of the parties. The ordinary courts are headed by the Court of Cassation, and administrative courts form a separate branch. The Supreme Constitutional Court reviews whether laws are constitutional. Economic courts hear many commercial and investment disputes. Arbitration is governed by Law No. 27 of 1994, and the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration (CRCICA) is the leading institution. Foreign judgments require exequatur, subject to reciprocity.

      Data protection

      Personal Data Protection Law No. 151 of 2020 and its Executive Regulations · Regulator: Personal Data Protection Centre (under the Ministry of Communications and Information Technology)

      Law 151 of 2020 requires controllers and processors to obtain a licence or permit from the Personal Data Protection Centre for many processing activities, including processing sensitive data and electronic marketing. Executive Regulations have now been issued. Transfers abroad are allowed only to countries with protection no lower than Egypt's, and need a Centre licence or permit unless an exception applies, such as express consent, legal claims or contract performance. For investigative work this means collecting only what is necessary, documenting the lawful basis, and planning in advance how and where personal data is reported.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified a published licensing regime that allows private investigators to carry out investigative activity in Egypt, and the legal position of independent private investigation is unclear. Several restrictions are clear. Surveillance, photographing certain sites, recording and approaching officials can raise security concerns. Inquiries into state and military entities are sensitive. Data protection licensing applies to personal data processing. Records held by government agencies, banks and the security services are not available for private inquiry. Engagements are therefore weighted toward documentary and open-source research, with any local activity scoped carefully with Egyptian counsel.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies are incorporated through the General Authority for Investment and Free Zones (GAFI), which runs investor service centres and an online incorporation portal, and they are entered in the commercial register. Commercial register extracts, which typically show a company's legal form, capital, managers and activity, are generally obtained through counsel or authorised agents rather than through an open public online search. We have not confirmed a public beneficial ownership register. Court judgments are only partly published, and real estate registration is known to be incomplete, so title research needs local expertise.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery of public officials is a criminal offence under the Penal Code. The Administrative Control Authority (ACA) is the principal anti-corruption and oversight body and carries out many corruption investigations and arrests. Anti-money laundering rules are set by the Anti-Money Laundering Law No. 80 of 2002, as amended, and the Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing Combating Unit serves as the financial intelligence unit. Egypt's AML/CFT regime was evaluated by MENAFATF, with on-site work in 2020. Prosecutions are brought by the Public Prosecution.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Egypt

      TDSC handles Egypt matters from Nashville. Because security sensitivities are significant, we scope each engagement conservatively. We first establish what can be answered from documents, including commercial register extracts obtained through counsel, published judgments, official gazettes, corporate disclosures, media in Arabic and English, and international records. Inquiries touching state or military entities are framed as lawful due diligence and nothing more. Where local verification is necessary, we coordinate qualified Egyptian professionals and the client's Egyptian counsel, and we avoid surveillance and any approach to officials. Data protection licensing and transfer restrictions under Law 151 of 2020 decide what personal data is collected, how much is collected, and how reporting to clients abroad is structured.

      TDSC has no office in Egypt. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Egypt Concentrates

      01

      Joint venture partner vetting

      Ownership, track record, litigation and reputational research on prospective Egyptian partners in oil and gas, real estate and manufacturing, where joint ventures are often required. The research pays particular attention to links with state-owned or military-owned enterprises and to politically exposed persons.

      02

      SCZone and logistics counterparties

      Due diligence on developers, contractors, freight forwarders and port service providers in the Suez Canal Economic Zone, including checks on corporate standing, beneficial control and adverse history before investment or supply contracts are signed.

      03

      Distributor and agent integrity

      Background and compliance reviews of commercial agents and distributors in regulated sectors such as pharmaceuticals, medical devices and telecoms, where licensing, government tenders and customs clearance create bribery risk under the FCPA or UK Bribery Act.

      04

      Construction and real estate disputes

      Fact-finding and counterparty research for disputes over large construction, new-city and hospitality projects, including subcontractor chains, payment defaults and the ownership history of land and units in a registration system with known gaps.

      05

      Asset tracing and award enforcement

      Identifying commercial interests, shareholdings and property of debtors to support counsel enforcing CRCICA or foreign arbitral awards, recognising that Egyptian enforcement procedures are slow and that much of the evidence must come from documents.

      Questions

      Egypt: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can company ownership in Egypt be verified?

      Usually in part. Commercial register extracts obtained through Egyptian counsel or authorised agents show the legal form, capital and managers, and joint stock companies may publish further disclosures. We have not confirmed a public beneficial ownership register. Ultimate control is often established by combining register data with corporate announcements, litigation, media and records in the investor's other jurisdictions.

      How does Law 151 of 2020 affect background research?

      It requires licences or permits from the Personal Data Protection Centre for many processing activities and restricts transfers of personal data outside Egypt. We design engagements to minimise the personal data collected, rely on documented lawful bases and exceptions, and work with counsel on whether a licence or a specific exception covers the reporting.

      Are private investigators licensed in Egypt?

      We have not identified a published licensing framework for private investigators in Egypt, and the legal position of independent private investigation is unclear. We therefore do not conduct surveillance or covert inquiries. Local work is limited to lawful documentary retrieval and interviews, carried out with qualified professionals and scoped with Egyptian counsel.

      What should we check about links to state or military entities?

      State-owned and military-affiliated companies are active in construction, manufacturing, food and other sectors, and private firms report that rules are applied to them unevenly. We identify such links through public ownership, contract and media sources so that clients can assess competitive, compliance and reputational implications. We do not attempt to obtain restricted information.

      Where are commercial disputes heard?

      Many contracts provide for arbitration under Law 27 of 1994, often at the Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration. Otherwise, commercial and investment disputes generally go to the economic courts, with further appeal to the Court of Cassation. Proceedings can be lengthy. Our role is evidential and factual support for counsel, not legal advice.
