      Overview

      Brazil at a Glance

      Brazil has about 212 million people (World Bank, 2024) and a civil law system organised under the 1988 Federal Constitution. Its federal structure shapes investigative work. Each state keeps its own Junta Comercial for company filings and its own state courts, while the federal tax authority maintains the national CNPJ register of legal entities. Data protection became a constitutional fundamental right in 2022, and the General Data Protection Law (LGPD) has been enforceable since 2021. Brazil also has a dedicated statute on private detectives, Law 13.432 of 2017, which frames what a private investigator may and may not do. Records are rich but fragmented, and most primary documents are in Portuguese.

      Organisations commission work involving Brazil because of its scale in agribusiness, mining, oil and gas, financial services, infrastructure and consumer markets, and because of its weight in regional supply chains. Typical exposures include counterparty and partner due diligence before acquisitions or distribution agreements, interaction with public bodies and state-owned companies, procurement and concession risk, asset tracing in support of judgments or arbitral awards, and fraud or diversion of funds by local partners. Clean Company Act exposure, labour and tax litigation history, and environmental or land-tenure issues in agribusiness and mining are recurring themes in pre-transaction research.

      Jurisdictional Framework

      The rules that govern lawful inquiry in Brazil

      Legal system

      Brazil has a civil law system based on the 1988 Federal Constitution, the 2002 Civil Code and the 2015 Code of Civil Procedure. Commercial disputes are generally heard by state courts, while federal courts handle matters involving the federal government. The Superior Court of Justice (STJ) unifies interpretation of federal law and the Federal Supreme Court (STF) decides constitutional questions. Some state courts, including São Paulo's, have specialised business and bankruptcy courts. Arbitration is well established under the Arbitration Law (Law 9.307/1996), and the CAM-CCBC in São Paulo is a leading institution.

      Data protection

      General Data Protection Law (LGPD, Law 13.709/2018) · Regulator: National Data Protection Authority (ANPD)

      The LGPD has applied since September 2020, with sanctions enforceable since August 2021. Lawful bases include legal obligation, the exercise of rights in legal proceedings, legitimate interests and credit protection, alongside consent. Sensitive data has narrower bases. Investigative work involving Brazilians or data collected in Brazil should document the purpose, necessity and lawful basis for each category of data. International transfers are governed by ANPD Resolution CD/ANPD 19/2024, which provides standard contractual clauses; since January 2026 Brazil and the EU recognise each other as adequate. No general data localisation requirement applies. Since September 2025 the ANPD has been an autonomous federal agency.

      Private investigators

      Brazil regulates the private detective profession through Law 13.432 of 2017. It describes the private detective as a professional who collects non-criminal data and information on matters of private interest to the client. A detective may assist a police investigation only with the client's express authorisation and the acceptance of the police authority in charge. We have not identified a licensing authority or register under the law. Detectives remain bound by constitutional privacy guarantees, the LGPD and the Criminal Code, including rules on interception and device intrusion. Bank, tax and telephone records are protected by secrecy rules and are not available for private inquiries.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies are registered with the Junta Comercial of the state where they are seated, under Law 8.934/1994 and the supervision of the national registration department (DREI). Articles, amendments, administrator appointments and partner changes are filed there, and certified copies are available. The Federal Revenue Service (Receita Federal) maintains the CNPJ register, issues registration and status certificates and publishes CNPJ open data. Beneficial ownership information is collected in the CNPJ system but is not, as far as we could verify, fully public. Court records are largely electronic and searchable by party name, subject to secrecy in family and some other matters. Real estate records are held by local registry offices.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      The Clean Company Act (Law 12.846/2013) imposes strict civil and administrative liability on companies for harmful acts against domestic or foreign public administrations and provides for leniency agreements. Individuals face liability under the Criminal Code and the Administrative Improbity Law. Money laundering is criminalised by Law 9.613/1998, as amended in 2012. The Comptroller General of the Union (CGU), federal and state prosecutors, the Federal Police and the financial intelligence unit COAF are the main enforcement and oversight bodies.

      Fact-finding where a Brazilian distributor, agent or local manager is suspected of diverting funds, operating related companies or misreporting sales, combining corporate record analysis with coordinated, lawful local inquiries carried out under the direction of counsel.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Brazil

      TDSC scopes Brazilian matters from Nashville, beginning with a jurisdictional review of which states, registries and courts are relevant, since company filings, court records and real estate records are held locally. We define the purpose and lawful basis for any personal data under the LGPD before research starts, and document how data will be transferred and stored. Open records research covers state Juntas Comerciais, CNPJ data and electronic court systems. Where fieldwork, interviews or document retrieval in Brazil are needed, we coordinate qualified local professionals operating within Law 13.432/2017 and the Criminal Code, and we work alongside Brazilian counsel on privilege, evidence and any enforcement strategy. We do not seek bank, tax or telephone records protected by secrecy.

      TDSC has no office in Brazil. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Brazil: Frequently Asked Questions

      Are private detectives regulated in Brazil?

      Yes. Law 13.432 of 2017 regulates the profession, describing it as the collection of non-criminal information on private matters for a client. It allows collaboration with police investigations only with client authorisation and police acceptance. As far as we could verify, it does not establish a licensing body, so TDSC selects local professionals on qualifications, references and compliance with the law.

      How do we find out who owns a Brazilian company?

      Start with the CNPJ register and the filings at the Junta Comercial of the state where the company is seated, which show partners and administrators for most limited companies. Corporations (S.A.) record shareholders in internal books rather than public filings. Beneficial ownership data collected by the Federal Revenue Service is not fully public, so layered structures often need further research.

      Does the LGPD prevent background checks on Brazilian individuals?

      No, but it shapes them. Research must have a lawful basis, such as legitimate interests or the exercise of rights in legal proceedings, and be limited to what is necessary for a stated purpose. Sensitive data such as health or political opinions has narrower bases. Transfers of results outside Brazil should rely on ANPD standard contractual clauses or another recognised mechanism.

      Can a foreign judgment or arbitral award be enforced against assets in Brazil?

      Foreign judgments and arbitral awards generally require recognition by the Superior Court of Justice before execution in Brazilian courts. Investigative work supports this by identifying real estate, corporate interests and litigation involving the debtor. Enforcement strategy, timing and any interim measures are matters for Brazilian counsel; TDSC provides the factual research that informs them.

      Why does Clean Company Act history matter in due diligence?

      The Clean Company Act imposes strict liability on companies for harmful acts against public administrations, and successor liability can follow mergers and acquisitions. Buyers and partners therefore look for prior leniency agreements, debarments, investigations and public contracts. TDSC reviews public sanctions lists, court records and reporting to identify these issues before a transaction closes.
