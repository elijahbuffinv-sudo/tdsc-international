      Overview

      Kazakhstan at a Glance

      Kazakhstan is a civil law jurisdiction that has been rewriting its institutions at pace. Voters approved a new constitution in a referendum on 15 March 2026, replacing the bicameral parliament with a unicameral Kurultai. Alongside the national courts, the Astana International Financial Centre operates its own common law court. Personal data must, as a rule, be stored in databases located in Kazakhstan, and processing generally depends on documented consent. Basic company registration data is open to any requester through the eGov portal, while the register of beneficial owners sits in a closed system used by state bodies. Each of these features shapes what can lawfully be gathered, from where, and how it can leave the country.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving Kazakhstan because of its weight in energy, mining and metals, its role as a transit and logistics corridor between China, Russia, the Caucasus and Europe, and the growth of the Astana International Financial Centre as a base for funds and holding structures. Typical exposures include opaque ownership behind contractors and joint-venture partners, state-linked counterparties, related-party transactions in resource projects, asset recovery following commercial disputes, and trade-compliance questions over goods moving through Central Asian routes. Counterparties may present well-documented corporate histories that still say little about who ultimately controls them.

      Law & Regulation

      What applies before any work touching Kazakhstan begins

      Legal system

      Kazakhstan has a civil law system built on codified legislation, including the Civil Code, the Criminal Code of 2014 and the Code on Administrative Offences of 2014, with a Supreme Court at the apex of the national courts. A new constitution was approved by referendum on 15 March 2026. The Astana International Financial Centre (AIFC) has a separate common law court based on English law principles, with exclusive jurisdiction over AIFC-related disputes and jurisdiction over others by party agreement, plus a Small Claims Court. The AIFC International Arbitration Centre (IAC) also operates in Astana.

      Data protection

      Law of the Republic of Kazakhstan No. 94-V on Personal Data and Their Protection (21 May 2013, as amended) · Regulator: Ministry of Digital Development, Innovations and Aerospace Industry (authorised body), with supervision by the prosecution authorities

      Collection and processing of personal data generally require the subject's consent, recorded in writing, through a state or non-state service, or by another verifiable method, with limited statutory exceptions. Processing must be limited to specific, legitimate purposes. Personal data is expected to be stored in databases located in Kazakhstan, which matters when material is gathered locally and reviewed abroad. Cross-border transfer is permitted to countries with adequate protection, and otherwise mainly with consent or under treaty or statutory exceptions. Legal entities must appoint a person responsible for data processing, and security incidents must be reported to the authorised body within one business day. Employee data is also governed by the Labour Code.

      Private investigators

      As of 2026, Kazakhstan has no enacted law specifically regulating private detective activity; the sector is not formally banned but operates without a dedicated framework. Earlier attempts at legislation in 1995, 2013 to 2015 and 2017 did not succeed. A draft law On Private Detective Activity was under public consultation by the Ministry of Internal Affairs in October 2025 and was taken up by the Mazhilis in March 2026. The draft contemplates licensing, insurance and strict experience requirements, and would bar covert operational-search activity, wiretapping and unauthorised covert filming, without granting access to state databases. Its final form and commencement remain to be confirmed.

      Corporate & public records

      Legal entities are recorded in the National Register of Business Identification Numbers, maintained under the Ministry of Justice. Basic registered data, including the business identification number, legal address and head of the executive body, can be requested by any person, and electronic extracts are available through the eGov portal; certified paper extracts come from Public Service Centres. Extracts for banks, insurers and securities market participants come through the financial market regulator. A register of beneficial owners exists under the anti-money laundering law, with rules approved by the Agency for Financial Monitoring in 2023, but it sits in a closed part of the authority's portal for state bodies and is not public.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      The Law on Countering Corruption of 18 November 2015 sets the preventive framework. The Criminal Code of 2014 criminalises bribery of public officials, including foreign public officials (Articles 366 to 368), and commercial bribery (Article 253). The Law on Countering the Legalisation (Laundering) of Proceeds from Crime and the Financing of Terrorism dates from 28 August 2009. In June 2025 the Anti-Corruption Agency was reorganised into an Anti-Corruption Service within the National Security Committee, with policy functions passing to the civil service agency. The Agency for Financial Monitoring leads on money laundering.

      Supporting foreign parents with Kazakh subsidiaries facing procurement irregularities, kickback allegations or conflicts of interest, with document review planned around consent-based data processing, local storage expectations and the Labour Code before any material is transferred abroad.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Kazakhstan

      TDSC scopes engagements touching Kazakhstan from Nashville, beginning with a jurisdictional review of what the client needs to establish and which sources can lawfully support it. Because the private detective sector has no enacted framework as of 2026, we do not rely on unregulated local operators; where work in country is required, we coordinate qualified professionals with appropriate standing and work alongside local counsel, particularly where evidence may be used before the national courts or the AIFC Court. We plan data handling around the 2013 personal data law, including consent, local storage expectations and cross-border transfer limits, before any personal data is collected or moved. We do not seek access to restricted state databases or the closed beneficial ownership register, and we do not use pretexting.

      Questions

      Kazakhstan: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can private investigators legally operate in Kazakhstan?

      Private detective work is not formally banned, but as of 2026 there is no enacted law regulating it. A draft law On Private Detective Activity was before the Mazhilis in 2026 and would introduce licensing and limits on covert methods. Until a law is in force, TDSC treats in-country fieldwork cautiously and scopes it with local counsel.

      Is beneficial ownership information in Kazakhstan publicly available?

      Not directly. A register of beneficial owners operates under the anti-money laundering law, but it sits in a closed part of the Agency for Financial Monitoring's portal and is used by state bodies. Ownership questions are therefore approached through public registry data, filings, court records and other lawful sources, with conclusions stated at the level the evidence supports.

      Can personal data gathered in Kazakhstan be reviewed in the United States?

      Possibly, but it must be planned. The 2013 personal data law expects personal data to be stored in databases in Kazakhstan and allows cross-border transfer to countries without adequate protection mainly with consent or under specific exceptions. TDSC works with local counsel to settle the lawful basis and transfer route before collection begins.

      How does the AIFC Court affect investigative work?

      The AIFC Court applies common law procedure based on English law and hears AIFC-related disputes and others where parties agree. Evidence prepared for it may face different expectations from evidence for the national courts. TDSC coordinates with counsel early so that research, witness contact and documentation suit the forum the client is likely to use.

      What can a basic Kazakh company extract tell us?

      An extract from the National Register of Business Identification Numbers typically confirms the entity's registration, business identification number, legal address and head of the executive body. It does not show beneficial ownership. TDSC uses extracts as a starting point and compares them with other lawful sources to build a fuller picture of control and history.
