      Overview

      Switzerland at a Glance

      Switzerland is a federal state of 26 cantons and sits outside the European Union and the EEA, so its rules differ from those of its neighbours in ways that matter for investigations. Data protection is governed by the revised Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP), in force since 1 September 2023 without a transition period, rather than by the GDPR. Company information comes from commercial registers kept by cantonal offices and indexed centrally through Zefix. Investigative activity is not regulated federally. Swiss law also treats certain information gathering on behalf of foreign parties, and the handing over of business secrets abroad, as matters of criminal law, so cross-border evidence collection is planned with Swiss counsel from the outset.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving Switzerland because of its weight as a financial, trading and holding location. Private banking and wealth management, commodity trading, pharmaceuticals, insurance and precision manufacturing are significant sectors, and many international groups hold regional headquarters, treasury functions or intellectual property in Swiss entities. Swiss companies, foundations and trust-related structures appear often in cross-border ownership chains. Typical engagements include due diligence on Swiss counterparties and their principals, asset tracing in support of litigation or award enforcement, integrity reviews of commodity trading intermediaries, and support for internal investigations at Swiss subsidiaries where employee data, banking secrecy and foreign-state concerns intersect.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Switzerland

      Legal system

      Switzerland has a civil law system built on federal codes, principally the Civil Code and the Code of Obligations, which contains company and contract law. Civil procedure is unified at federal level, but courts of first instance and appeal are cantonal. Several cantons, including Zurich, Bern, St. Gallen and Aargau, maintain specialised commercial courts for business disputes. The Federal Supreme Court in Lausanne is the final instance. Switzerland is a leading arbitral seat; the Swiss Arbitration Centre administers cases under the Swiss Rules of International Arbitration, revised in 2021.

      Data protection

      Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP) of 25 September 2020, in force 1 September 2023, with the Data Protection Ordinance (DPO) · Regulator: Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC)

      The FADP protects natural persons only and reaches foreign businesses that process data affecting Switzerland. Investigative processing must be lawful, proportionate and purpose-limited, and data on criminal proceedings is treated as sensitive. Transfers abroad are permitted to countries on the Federal Council's list in Annex 1 to the DPO, which includes EEA states and the United Kingdom; other destinations need safeguards such as adapted EU Standard Contractual Clauses. Since 15 September 2024, certified US companies can receive data under the Swiss-US Data Privacy Framework. Remote access from abroad to data held in Switzerland counts as a transfer. Wilful breaches of certain duties can lead to criminal fines of up to CHF 250,000, aimed primarily at responsible individuals.

      Private investigators

      Switzerland has no federal licensing regime for private investigators. Regulation of security and investigation businesses is a cantonal matter and varies: some cantons, notably in French-speaking Switzerland, require authorisation for such activities, while others impose no specific licence. Swiss investigators hold no state powers. Their work is constrained by the FADP, by civil law protection of personality rights and by the Swiss Criminal Code, which penalises unauthorised recording of conversations and certain intrusions into private life. We have not confirmed the current cantonal requirements from primary sources, so the position for any particular canton is checked with local counsel before fieldwork is scoped.

      Corporate & public records

      The commercial register is kept by cantonal or district commercial register offices and can be searched centrally through Zefix. Entries are published in the Swiss Official Gazette of Commerce (SHAB/FOSC), issued by the State Secretariat for Economic Affairs. Extracts and supporting documents are public and show the company's name, seat, purpose, capital, governing bodies, auditors and authorised signatories. Partners of a GmbH are entered in the register; shareholders of an AG are not. Switzerland has not historically operated a central beneficial ownership register. Legislation on the transparency of legal entities has been in progress, but we have not confirmed its current status or access rules.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery of Swiss and foreign public officials and private sector bribery are offences under the Swiss Criminal Code, and companies can be held liable where an offence cannot be attributed to an individual because of organisational deficiencies. The Anti-Money Laundering Act imposes due diligence and reporting duties on financial intermediaries, with suspicious activity reports going to the Money Laundering Reporting Office (MROS). The Office of the Attorney General of Switzerland prosecutes major cross-border cases, and FINMA supervises financial institutions.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Switzerland Concentrates

      01

      Counterparty and principal due diligence

      Swiss holding, trading and service companies sit in many international ownership chains. Due diligence combines cantonal register extracts, SHAB publications, litigation and debt enforcement records where obtainable, and media in German, French and Italian to establish who controls a counterparty and how it operates.

      02

      Commodity trading intermediary reviews

      Geneva, Zug and Lugano host a substantial share of global commodity trading. Engagements screen traders, agents and their beneficial owners for integrity concerns, sanctions-adjacent ownership and links to politically exposed persons in producing countries before contracts or financing are agreed.

      03

      Asset tracing for enforcement

      Creditors pursuing judgments or arbitral awards often need to establish whether a debtor holds companies, real property or other interests in Switzerland. TDSC maps what lawful public sources show and works with Swiss counsel on attachment and disclosure routes available through the courts.

      04

      Internal investigations at Swiss entities

      Inquiries into fraud or misconduct at Swiss subsidiaries must account for employee data protection, banking and professional secrecy and criminal law limits on passing information to foreign authorities. Review plans and data flows are agreed with Swiss counsel before documents are collected or interviews held.

      05

      Wealth structure and family office reviews

      Foundations, family offices and layered holding structures can separate legal title from control. Reviews reconstruct ownership and governance from register filings, published notices and court material, and identify where further information is only obtainable through counsel or legal process.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Switzerland

      TDSC begins by identifying which cantons, languages and regulated sectors a matter touches, and whether any information is to be used by foreign courts or authorities, because Swiss criminal law restricts some forms of information gathering for foreign parties. Desk research uses commercial register extracts, SHAB notices, court publications, media archives and reputable commercial databases. Anything beyond public-record work, such as local enquiries or observation, is scoped with Swiss counsel and coordinated with professionals authorised in the relevant canton. Bank, employee and client data is sought only through channels counsel confirms are lawful. Personal data is minimised and documented under the FADP, and transfers to Nashville rely on a recognised mechanism such as the Swiss-US Data Privacy Framework or contractual clauses.

      TDSC has no office in Switzerland. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Switzerland: Frequently Asked Questions

      Do private investigators need a licence in Switzerland?

      There is no federal licence. Requirements are set by the cantons and differ between them; some require authorisation for investigation or security businesses and others do not. TDSC does not hold a Swiss authorisation. Where work on the ground is needed, it coordinates with professionals authorised in the relevant canton after checking the local position with counsel.

      Does the GDPR apply to investigations in Switzerland?

      Switzerland is outside the EU, so its own Federal Act on Data Protection applies, in revised form since 1 September 2023. The GDPR may still apply in parallel where a matter involves processing connected with the EU. The FADP has its own transfer rules, and data sent to the United States can rely on the Swiss-US Data Privacy Framework for certified recipients.

      Can we see who owns a Swiss company?

      Partly. The commercial register shows directors, signatories and capital, and lists the partners of a GmbH, but does not show the shareholders of an AG. Switzerland has not historically had a public beneficial ownership register, so ownership of an AG is usually reconstructed from filings, published notices, litigation material and other lawful sources.

      Why is Swiss counsel involved so early?

      Swiss criminal law restricts certain acts performed for a foreign state and the transfer of business secrets abroad, and banking and professional secrecy add further limits. Evidence collected without regard to these rules can expose the people involved and may not be usable. Counsel confirms what may be gathered, by whom and how it may leave Switzerland.

      Which language will records be in?

      It depends on the canton. Register entries, court decisions and local media may be in German, French or Italian, and sometimes more than one for the same company. TDSC works with source-language material and provides translations or summaries, noting where a certified translation is needed for court or regulatory use.
