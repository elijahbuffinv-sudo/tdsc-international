      Overview

      Australia at a Glance

      Australia is a common law federation with six states and two self-governing territories. That structure shapes almost every investigative engagement. Companies are registered nationally with the Australian Securities and Investments Commission (ASIC), and privacy obligations come mainly from the Commonwealth Privacy Act 1988. Investigator licensing, surveillance devices, land titles and much of the court system, however, are matters for each state and territory. A matter touching Sydney and Perth can therefore fall under two different licensing regimes and two different surveillance statutes. Reforms in 2024 extended the privacy law, including a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, and broadened foreign bribery liability for companies. Investigative methods that were routine a few years ago need to be checked against current law.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving Australia for several reasons. The country is a major exporter of resources and agricultural products, a significant destination for foreign investment, and the home of a large superannuation and funds management sector. Typical engagements include pre-transaction due diligence on unlisted Australian companies and trusts, asset tracing in support of judgments or insolvency, and enquiries into supplier or distributor conduct in the Asia-Pacific region. Litigation support for proceedings in the Federal Court and state Supreme Courts is also common. Since 2024 Australian companies have faced strict liability for failing to prevent foreign bribery by their associates. As a result, demand has risen for third-party and agent due diligence in Southeast Asia and the Pacific.

      Law & Regulation

      What applies before any work touching Australia begins

      Legal system

      Australia has a common law system with a written federal Constitution. Commonwealth legislation governs corporations, competition, consumer protection and privacy. The states and territories legislate on property, much of contract and tort practice, and licensing of occupations. Commercial disputes are heard in the Federal Court of Australia and in the state Supreme Courts, several of which run dedicated commercial lists. The High Court of Australia is the final court of appeal. International arbitration is supported by the International Arbitration Act 1974 and administered by bodies including the Australian Centre for International Commercial Arbitration (ACICA).

      Data protection

      Privacy Act 1988 (Cth), including the Australian Privacy Principles, as amended by the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024 · Regulator: Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC)

      The Privacy Act binds most organisations with annual turnover above AUD 3 million, as well as certain smaller businesses, through the Australian Privacy Principles. Collection must be reasonably necessary, by lawful and fair means, and generally from the individual concerned unless that is unreasonable or impracticable. The 2024 amendments, assented to on 10 December 2024, strengthened the OAIC's enforcement powers. They also created a statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy, which commenced in mid-2025. The employee records exemption remains in place as of 2025, but a second tranche of reforms is expected. Cross-border disclosures generally leave the disclosing entity accountable for the overseas recipient's handling. The amendments add a mechanism for prescribing countries with substantially similar protection.

      Private investigators

      Private investigators are licensed at state and territory level, and requirements differ. In New South Wales, NSW Police issue Class 2E licences under the Security Industry Act 1997, and the Security Licensing and Enforcement Directorate oversees the industry. In Western Australia, WA Police Licensing Services administer investigator licences under the Security and Related Activities (Control) Act 1996. Published commentary indicates the Australian Capital Territory does not license investigators. Each jurisdiction also has its own surveillance devices legislation that restricts recording private conversations and installing tracking or optical devices. Courts have excluded covertly obtained footage. Investigators may not impersonate police, and stalking and tracking offences apply to them in full.

      Corporate & public records

      ASIC maintains the national companies register. For a fee, searches return current and historical extracts showing directors, company secretaries, registered office and share structure. Lodged documents are also available, with fuller financial reports for large proprietary and public companies. The Australian Business Register provides ABN details without charge. Unlisted companies keep their own registers of members, but as of October 2025 there is no public central beneficial ownership register. The government has announced a public, Commonwealth-operated register for unlisted companies, with stakeholder engagement planned from early 2027. Land titles are held by state and territory registries, and court records are searchable to varying degrees in each jurisdiction.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Foreign bribery is an offence under the Criminal Code Act 1995. The Crimes Legislation Amendment (Combatting Foreign Bribery) Act 2024 added a strict liability offence of failing to prevent foreign bribery by associates, effective 8 September 2024, with an adequate procedures defence. The Australian Federal Police and the Commonwealth Director of Public Prosecutions enforce these offences. The National Anti-Corruption Commission has investigated Commonwealth public sector corruption since 1 July 2023. AUSTRAC administers the Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act 2006, which was extended to lawyers, accountants and real estate professionals from 1 July 2026.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Australia

      TDSC starts by identifying every state and territory the matter touches, because licensing and surveillance rules differ between them. Work begins with ASIC extracts and lodged documents, Australian Business Register data, insolvency and court records, and state title searches. Where enquiries on the ground, interviews or surveillance are needed, TDSC coordinates investigators licensed in the relevant jurisdiction and checks the proposed methods against that state's surveillance devices law. Matters headed for litigation or regulatory proceedings are scoped with Australian counsel so that evidence is gathered in an admissible form. Personal information is collected only as far as the purpose requires, handled in line with the Australian Privacy Principles, and stored under documented controls in Nashville.

      TDSC has no office in Australia. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Australia Concentrates

      01

      Due diligence on unlisted entities

      Proprietary companies and trusts are widely used in Australian deal structures, and no public beneficial ownership register exists yet. Ownership work therefore combines ASIC extracts, lodged documents, court and insolvency records, title searches and open-source enquiry. Where a chain of control runs through discretionary trusts, the work often needs direct confirmation from the parties.

      02

      Third-party bribery risk reviews

      The failure to prevent offence makes Australian companies answer for associates' conduct overseas. Engagements review agents, joint venture partners and intermediaries, particularly in resources, infrastructure and agriculture across Southeast Asia and the Pacific. The aim is to document the adequate procedures a company may need to rely on.

      03

      Asset tracing and enforcement support

      Creditors enforcing judgments or arbitral awards in Australia need evidence of real property, company interests and other assets across several states. TDSC works with Australian counsel on what can be found through public registers and what requires court orders such as freezing orders or examinations.

      04

      Workplace and misconduct investigations

      Allegations of fraud, harassment or leaks inside an Australian subsidiary call for procedural fairness and care with surveillance laws that differ by state. Privacy obligations apply alongside the employee records exemption. TDSC helps foreign parent companies structure investigations so that findings will hold up in Fair Work or court proceedings.

      05

      Resources and supply chain enquiries

      Mining, energy and critical minerals projects draw international capital and partners. Engagements include background reviews of project counterparties, histories of tenement ownership and disputes, regulatory enforcement history, and reputational screening ahead of offtake, financing or joint venture agreements.

      Questions

      Australia: Frequently Asked Questions

      Does a private investigator need a licence in every Australian state?

      Licensing is set at state and territory level, and the rules differ. New South Wales issues investigator licences through NSW Police under the Security Industry Act 1997, and Western Australia uses WA Police Licensing Services. Published commentary suggests the ACT does not license investigators. TDSC uses investigators licensed in the jurisdiction where the work takes place.

      Can we find out who really owns an Australian proprietary company?

      An ASIC extract shows registered members and officers, but as of 2025 there is no public central register of beneficial owners. Nominee holdings and trusts can hide control. The government has announced a public register for unlisted companies, but detailed work is not due to start until 2027. Until then, ownership is pieced together from several sources and may need confirmation through due diligence requests.

      How does the new statutory privacy tort affect investigations?

      Since mid-2025, individuals can sue over serious invasions of privacy, such as intrusion upon seclusion or misuse of information, where the invasion was intentional or reckless and no public interest outweighs it. Covert methods carry more legal risk as a result. TDSC reviews methods with local counsel before any surveillance or intrusive enquiry.

      Can covert recordings be used in Australian proceedings?

      This depends on the state's surveillance devices law and on the court's discretion. In Kadir v The Queen (2020), the High Court upheld the exclusion of covert footage taken on private premises without consent. TDSC does not arrange recordings that the applicable legislation prohibits, and it treats admissibility as a question for counsel.

      What does the failure to prevent foreign bribery offence mean for our due diligence?

      Since 8 September 2024, an Australian company can be liable if an associate bribes a foreign official for its benefit, unless it had adequate procedures in place. Documented, proportionate due diligence on agents, intermediaries and partners is therefore central evidence of those procedures, especially in higher-risk markets.
