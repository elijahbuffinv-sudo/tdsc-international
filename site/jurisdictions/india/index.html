      Investigations Touching India

      Investigative consulting for matters touching India means working across a federal system of 28 states, a large and partly digitised public record, and an investigation sector without a national licensing statute: corporate and promoter research, litigation and asset tracing for counsel, and carefully vetted local fieldwork.

      Overview

      India at a Glance

      India is a common law federation with a large, fast-growing economy, an extensive body of corporate and regulatory filings, and courts that are heavily used for commercial disputes. Much useful information is public, including company filings with the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, court orders and regulatory notices, but records are spread across central, state and district systems and vary in quality. The private investigation sector is large and uneven: Parliament considered a Private Detective Agencies (Regulation) Bill in 2007 but withdrew it in 2020, so there is no national licensing regime. Privacy law is in transition, with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 being brought into force in phases through May 2027.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving India because of its scale as a sourcing, technology services, manufacturing and investment destination. Typical exposures include promoter and related-party risk in family-controlled groups, inflated or fictitious vendors, distributor and channel fraud, intellectual property leakage and counterfeiting, bribery risk in licensing and procurement, and integrity concerns about local partners in joint ventures. Investors and lenders seek background on promoters before transactions, and claimants pursue assets and corporate interests after awards or judgments. Because outcomes often depend on the reliability of local enquiries, the choice and supervision of field resources matter as much as the research itself.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving India

      Legal system

      India has a common law system within a written federal Constitution, with codified statutes on contract, companies, evidence and procedure. The Supreme Court sits above 25 High Courts, several of which exercise original commercial jurisdiction. The Commercial Courts Act 2015, amended in 2018, created commercial courts at district level and commercial divisions and appellate divisions in High Courts, and introduced mandatory pre-institution mediation for suits not seeking urgent relief. Arbitration is governed by the Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996, and institutions include the India International Arbitration Centre in New Delhi and the Mumbai Centre for International Arbitration.

      Data protection

      Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025, being brought into force in phases; Information Technology Act, 2000 and its 2011 privacy rules in the interim · Regulator: Data Protection Board of India

      The DPDP Act and Rules were notified on 13 November 2025, with provisions establishing the Data Protection Board in force immediately, consent manager provisions due on 13 November 2026 and the main obligations on 13 May 2027. Until then the IT Act and its privacy rules continue to apply. The Act relies on consent and listed legitimate uses, including processing for employment purposes and to protect an employer from loss or liability. Cross-border transfers are generally permitted except to countries the government restricts, while sector rules such as Reserve Bank of India payment data requirements impose localisation. Investigative plans should anticipate the 2027 obligations now.

      Private investigators

      India has no national statute licensing private investigators or detective agencies. The Private Detective Agencies (Regulation) Bill 2007, introduced by the Ministry of Home Affairs and examined by a parliamentary standing committee, would have created central and state licensing boards, but it was withdrawn in the Rajya Sabha in March 2020. Investigators therefore operate under general law, including the IT Act, criminal provisions on trespass, stalking and impersonation, and the constitutional right to privacy recognised by the Supreme Court. Obtaining call records, bank statements or government identity data without lawful authority is unlawful, and the quality of local agencies varies widely, so vetting and supervision are essential.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies and limited liability partnerships are registered with the Registrar of Companies under the Ministry of Corporate Affairs, and the MCA21 portal provides company master data, director details, charges and, for a fee, filed documents such as annual returns and financial statements. Annual returns list shareholders. Companies must identify significant beneficial owners and file declarations with the Registrar under the Companies (Significant Beneficial Owners) Rules 2018, but this information is not offered as a freely searchable public register. Court orders are available through the eCourts services and High Court websites, and land records are maintained by individual states with varying digitisation.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      The Prevention of Corruption Act 1988, substantially amended in 2018, criminalises bribery of public servants, makes bribe-giving a direct offence and introduces liability for commercial organisations whose associates bribe to obtain or retain business. The Prevention of Money Laundering Act 2002 and the Fugitive Economic Offenders Act are enforced by the Directorate of Enforcement, which also enforces foreign exchange law. The Central Bureau of Investigation, the Central Vigilance Commission and the Lokpal, established under the Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013, address public-sector corruption.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching India

      TDSC scopes India matters from Nashville by defining the question, the states and courts likely to matter, and the evidential standard the client needs. Desk research draws on MCA21 filings, court records, regulatory orders and lawful open sources, including regional-language media where relevant. Because India has no licensing statute for investigators, TDSC applies its own vetting before coordinating any local professional: track record, references, methods, conflict checks and written limits that exclude call records, bank data, identity databases and pretexting. Indian counsel is engaged on privacy, criminal law and evidential questions. Personal data is handled with the DPDP Act's phased obligations in view, transferred under contract and kept to the engagement's defined purpose.

      TDSC has no office in India. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving India Concentrates

      01

      Promoter and related-party diligence

      Researching founders and promoter families behind Indian groups, including directorships across MCA records, related-party companies, litigation in High Courts and district courts, regulatory orders from SEBI and other bodies, and media coverage in English and regional languages, before investment, lending or joint venture decisions.

      02

      Vendor and distributor fraud

      Supporting internal investigations into fictitious or conflicted vendors, inflated invoicing and channel stuffing by distributors, by testing supplier existence and ownership against corporate filings and lawful site verification, and by identifying links between suppliers and employees responsible for their selection.

      03

      Counterfeiting and IP leakage

      Assisting brand owners and counsel with market enquiries into counterfeit goods, grey-market diversion and misuse of confidential technology by former staff or licensees, with fieldwork carried out by vetted local professionals and evidence preserved for civil or criminal proceedings brought by Indian counsel.

      04

      Bribery risk in licensing and procurement

      Reviewing intermediaries, consultants and liaison agents used to obtain permits, land approvals or public contracts, given the commercial organisation offence introduced by the 2018 amendments to the Prevention of Corruption Act and the exposure of foreign parents under their own anti-bribery laws.

      05

      Asset tracing and enforcement support

      Identifying corporate shareholdings, property interests and related entities connected to a judgment or award debtor, using MCA filings, charge records, court records and lawful open sources, so that Indian counsel can assess attachment, insolvency or other enforcement routes.

      Questions

      India: Frequently Asked Questions

      Do private investigators in India need a licence?

      There is no national licensing law for private investigators in India. A Private Detective Agencies (Regulation) Bill was introduced in 2007 but was withdrawn in 2020. Some agencies hold registrations for other activities, but that is not a licence to investigate. TDSC therefore vets any Indian professional it coordinates on methods, references and conflicts, and sets written limits on what may be done.

      Is the DPDP Act in force yet?

      Partly. The DPDP Act 2023 and the DPDP Rules 2025 were notified on 13 November 2025. Provisions establishing the Data Protection Board took effect then, consent manager provisions follow on 13 November 2026, and the main compliance obligations apply from 13 May 2027. Until then the Information Technology Act 2000 and its privacy rules continue to govern. Engagements running into 2027 should be designed for the new regime.

      What can be learned from MCA company records?

      MCA21 shows a company's registration details, status, directors and their other directorships, and registered charges, and filed documents such as annual returns and financial statements can be obtained for a fee. Annual returns list shareholders. Filings can be late or incomplete, especially for smaller private companies, so TDSC cross-checks them against court records, regulatory orders and other sources before drawing conclusions about control or financial health.

      Can TDSC obtain call records or bank statements in India?

      No. Call detail records, bank account information and government identity data are protected, and obtaining them without lawful authority is an offence. Such records can be sought only through court processes or by authorities. TDSC does not obtain them and does not coordinate anyone who offers to. Indian counsel can advise whether disclosure or production orders are available in a particular proceeding.

      How are findings prepared for Indian proceedings?

      Findings are reported with each source identified and with verified facts separated from inference. Electronic material relied on in Indian courts is subject to authentication requirements under the evidence law, so collection details are recorded at the time. TDSC works with Indian counsel, who decide how findings are used, whether affidavits or witness evidence are needed and how they fit commercial court or arbitration procedure.
