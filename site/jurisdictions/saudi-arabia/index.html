      Saudi Arabia: Investigative Consulting

      Investigative consulting for matters touching Saudi Arabia means scoping work around a Sharia-based legal system undergoing rapid codification, a new personal data law enforced by SDAIA, strict limits on surveillance and image capture, and counterparty questions raised by Vision 2030 contracting, joint ventures and regional headquarters.

      Overview

      Saudi Arabia at a Glance

      Saudi Arabia's legal environment combines Sharia as the foundational source of law with an expanding body of codified statutes. Recent years have brought a Civil Transactions Law (in force since December 2022), an Evidence Law and a Personal Status Law (both in force since May 2022), restructured commercial courts and the Personal Data Protection Law, enforced since September 2024. The Anti-Cyber Crime Law of 2007 criminalises invasion of privacy through the misuse of camera-equipped devices, which bears directly on surveillance and photography. We have not identified a licensing regime for private investigators, so investigative work is framed as research, advisory and counsel-led evidence work rather than field investigation.

      Organisations commission work involving Saudi Arabia because of the scale of Vision 2030 spending on infrastructure, tourism, entertainment, mining, logistics, healthcare and renewable energy, and giga-projects such as NEOM, Qiddiya, the Red Sea Project and Diriyah Gate. Government-linked contracting, the regional headquarters programme and local-content and Saudization requirements bring foreign companies into partnerships with Saudi contractors, agents and family-owned groups. Typical exposures include agent and intermediary integrity, subcontractor capacity and payment risk, joint-venture disputes, enforcement of awards and judgments, and reputational questions about counterparties connected to state entities or prominent families.

      Jurisdictional Framework

      The rules that govern lawful inquiry in Saudi Arabia

      Legal system

      Saudi Arabia's legal system is founded on Islamic law (Sharia), supplemented by royal decrees and codified statutes. The Ministry of Justice oversees the general court system, including commercial courts restructured under the Commercial Courts Law approved in 2020, while the Board of Grievances hears disputes involving government entities and contractors. Enforcement Courts execute domestic and recognised foreign judgments and arbitral awards under the 2012 Enforcement Law. The Saudi Center for Commercial Arbitration (SCCA), established in 2016, administers commercial arbitration. Proceedings are in Arabic.

      Data protection

      Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL), Royal Decree M/19 of 2021 as amended by Royal Decree M/148 of 2023, with its Implementing Regulations and Transfer Regulations · Regulator: Saudi Data and Artificial Intelligence Authority (SDAIA)

      The PDPL took effect on 14 September 2023, with most controllers required to comply by 14 September 2024. It applies to processing of personal data relating to individuals in Saudi Arabia, including by entities abroad. Sensitive data is broadly defined and includes tribal origin, religious and political beliefs, criminal and security data, credit, health and location data. Transfers abroad are permitted for specified purposes to jurisdictions with adequate protection or under appropriate safeguards, with data limited to the minimum necessary. Breaches must be notified to SDAIA within 72 hours, and harmful disclosure of sensitive data can carry imprisonment. The Saudi Central Bank and telecoms regulator retain sectoral rules.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified a statute or licensing scheme that authorises private investigation as a commercial activity in Saudi Arabia. Several laws constrain investigative methods. The Anti-Cyber Crime Law (2007) makes invasion of privacy through misuse of camera-equipped mobile phones and similar devices, and defamation through information technology, offences punishable by up to one year's imprisonment and fines of up to SAR 500,000. The PDPL restricts collection and disclosure of personal data, particularly sensitive data. Surveillance, covert photography and pretext enquiries are therefore unsuitable, and engagements are scoped with Saudi counsel.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies and establishments are registered in the Commercial Register administered by the Ministry of Commerce, which offers online enquiry of commercial registration data and verification of documents issued through its systems. A registration record typically confirms the entity's name, registration number, legal form, status and main activity; the depth of officer and shareholder detail available to third parties varies and is often confirmed through the counterparty or Saudi counsel. Listed companies publish disclosures on the Saudi Exchange. Open Ownership records Saudi Arabia as implementing beneficial ownership reform; we have not identified a publicly searchable beneficial ownership register.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      The Combating Bribery Law and the Civil Service Law are the principal anti-corruption statutes; the Combating Bribery Law was amended in December 2021 to criminalise bribery of foreign public officials. The Oversight and Anti-Corruption Authority (Nazaha), restructured by royal decrees in December 2019 and reporting to the King, leads enforcement. The Anti-Money Laundering Law is administered with the Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) and the Saudi Arabian Financial Intelligence Unit (SAFIU), with prosecution by the Public Prosecution.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Saudi Arabia Concentrates

      01

      Agent and intermediary integrity

      Researching commercial agents, consultants and introducers used to win government-linked or giga-project contracts, including registration status, ownership signals, connections to public officials or state entities, and prior adverse reporting, to support anti-bribery compliance under the FCPA and the Saudi Combating Bribery Law.

      02

      Contractor and subcontractor vetting

      Assessing Saudi contractors and suppliers on construction, infrastructure and Vision 2030 projects for registration, track record, disputes, payment history signals and capacity, where delays, subcontracting chains and Saudization compliance can affect delivery and expose foreign primes to cost and reputational risk.

      03

      Joint-venture partner due diligence

      Background research on family-owned groups and local partners before joint ventures, regional headquarters arrangements or minority investments, covering corporate history, principal individuals as publicly reported, litigation and regulatory matters, and media coverage in Arabic and English, scoped to PDPL limits on personal data.

      04

      Award and judgment enforcement support

      Assembling lawful public information on a Saudi counterparty's registered entities, affiliates, listed holdings and publicly reported assets to help counsel assess whether enforcing an arbitral award or foreign judgment through the Enforcement Courts is practical, and where recoverable value may sit.

      05

      Reputational and politically exposed screening

      Screening counterparties for connections to state-owned entities, sovereign investment vehicles and senior officials, together with sanctions and adverse media in Arabic, to support onboarding, investment committee decisions and lender due diligence, while recognising that public information about senior figures is limited and carefully handled.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Saudi Arabia

      TDSC scopes Saudi Arabia matters from Nashville with Saudi counsel involved early, because the absence of an identified investigator licensing regime, the Anti-Cyber Crime Law and the PDPL together rule out surveillance, covert photography and pretext enquiries. Work concentrates on lawful sources: Ministry of Commerce registration data, Saudi Exchange disclosures, published regulatory actions, court information obtained through counsel, and Arabic and English media read by Arabic-literate researchers. Where documents must be retrieved or verified locally, TDSC coordinates appropriately qualified local professionals under counsel's direction. Personal data about Saudi residents is minimised, sensitive categories are avoided unless strictly necessary and lawful, and any transfer is assessed against the PDPL Transfer Regulations.

      TDSC has no office in Saudi Arabia. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Saudi Arabia: Frequently Asked Questions

      Are private investigators licensed in Saudi Arabia?

      We have not identified a licensing scheme that authorises private investigation as a commercial activity in Saudi Arabia. Methods common elsewhere, such as surveillance or covert photography, risk offences under the Anti-Cyber Crime Law and breaches of the PDPL. TDSC therefore provides research and advisory work from the United States and leaves evidence-gathering in Saudi proceedings to Saudi counsel.

      Does the Saudi PDPL apply to research carried out from outside the Kingdom?

      The PDPL applies to processing of personal data relating to individuals in Saudi Arabia, including by entities outside the Kingdom. Research about Saudi residents conducted from the United States should therefore have a defined purpose, collect only what is necessary and avoid sensitive categories such as tribal origin, religious beliefs, criminal records or location data unless clearly lawful.

      How can a Saudi company's registration be confirmed?

      The Ministry of Commerce offers online enquiry of commercial registration data and a document verification service. These confirm that an entity is registered and identify its registration number, legal form and status. Ownership and signatory details may need to be confirmed with the counterparty, through Saudi counsel or, for listed companies, through Saudi Exchange disclosures.

      What is the most common investigative need in Vision 2030 projects?

      Foreign contractors and investors most often need confidence about the people they are paying: agents, consultants, subcontractors and local partners. Questions centre on whether an intermediary is genuine, whether it has connections to officials or state entities, and whether a subcontractor can deliver. These are answered through registration checks, public-record and media research, and documented compliance analysis.

      Can TDSC help with enforcing an award against a Saudi party?

      TDSC can assemble lawful public information on a Saudi counterparty's registered entities, affiliates and publicly reported holdings to help counsel judge whether enforcement through the Saudi Enforcement Courts is worthwhile. TDSC does not access bank, land or government databases and does not provide legal advice on enforcement, which remains with Saudi and international counsel.
