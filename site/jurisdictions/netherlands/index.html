      Overview

      Netherlands at a Glance

      The Netherlands is an EU member state with a civil law tradition and a dense, well-documented corporate environment. Personal data is governed by the GDPR and the Uitvoeringswet AVG (UAVG), supervised by the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens, which has been an active enforcer. Private investigation is a regulated activity: agencies operate under a licensing system administered for the Minister of Justice and Security, and investigators must meet qualification requirements. Company information is held in the Business Register of the Netherlands Chamber of Commerce (KVK), which also maintains the UBO register. For international disputes, the Netherlands Commercial Court in Amsterdam has heard cases in English since 2019. Taken together, the environment is transparent in many respects but sets clear limits on how personal data may be gathered and used.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving the Netherlands largely because of its role as a corporate and logistics hub. Rotterdam and Schiphol anchor trade flows into Europe, and Dutch holding, financing and licensing companies sit in a large share of multinational group structures. Agri-food, chemicals, energy, semiconductors and financial services are significant sectors. Typical engagements include due diligence on Dutch holding companies and the operating groups beneath them, asset tracing in support of litigation or award enforcement, supply chain and customs integrity reviews linked to the ports, and internal investigations at Dutch subsidiaries, where works council rights and the UAVG shape how evidence is gathered.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Netherlands Concentrates

      01

      Holding company due diligence

      Dutch holding, financing and licensing companies sit in many international group structures. Due diligence links KVK extracts, deposited financial statements and court records with enquiries into the operating companies beneath them, which is usually where the material exposures lie.

      02

      Port and supply chain integrity

      Rotterdam and the wider logistics sector draw counterparties ranging from established forwarders to thinly capitalised intermediaries. Engagements screen logistics partners, importers and their principals for integrity concerns, sanctions-adjacent ownership and links to customs or cargo fraud before contracts are signed.

      03

      Ownership tracing after UBO restrictions

      With public access to Dutch UBO data suspended since 2022, ownership is reconstructed from KVK filings, deposited accounts, Enterprise Chamber and other court material, media archives and, where a party qualifies, a properly made request through Dutch counsel.

      04

      Works council aware internal investigations

      Inquiries into misconduct at Dutch subsidiaries must respect employee data protection and the works council's role in monitoring arrangements. TDSC helps foreign parent companies plan document review and interviews with Dutch counsel and a licensed agency so that findings remain usable.

      05

      Asset tracing for enforcement

      Creditors enforcing judgments or arbitral awards often need to locate Dutch companies, real property or receivables. TDSC maps what KVK, Kadaster and court sources show and works with Dutch counsel on prejudgment attachment and enforcement routes available through the courts.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Netherlands

      Legal system

      The Netherlands has a civil law system based on codified statutes, principally the Civil Code (Burgerlijk Wetboek), which contains company and contract law, and the Code of Civil Procedure. Commercial disputes go to the district courts, with appeals to the courts of appeal and cassation to the Supreme Court (Hoge Raad). The Netherlands Commercial Court (NCC), part of the Amsterdam courts since 1 January 2019, conducts proceedings and gives judgments in English, with an NCC Court of Appeal. The Enterprise Chamber of the Amsterdam Court of Appeal hears corporate inquiry proceedings.

      Data protection

      General Data Protection Regulation (EU) 2016/679 and the Dutch GDPR Implementation Act (Uitvoeringswet AVG, UAVG) · Regulator: Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (Dutch Data Protection Authority)

      Investigative processing needs a GDPR lawful basis, usually legitimate interests or the establishment and defence of legal claims, and must be proportionate. Data on criminal convictions and offences may be processed only under the exceptions in the UAVG, which include legal claims and explicit consent; the Dutch definition also covers court-imposed prohibitions. A national identification number may be processed only where a law expressly allows it, and only for that law's purposes. Transfers outside the EEA require an adequacy decision or safeguards such as the 2021 Standard Contractual Clauses with a transfer impact assessment. Certified US recipients may rely on the EU-US Data Privacy Framework.

      Private investigators

      Private investigation agencies (particuliere recherchebureaus) must hold a licence under the Private Security Organisations and Detective Agencies Act (Wpbr), granted on behalf of the Minister of Justice and Security, with Justis handling licensing and qualification matters. Licensed agencies are expected to follow the sector's privacy code of conduct, which governs how investigations are carried out and how personal data is handled. Individual investigators must meet qualification and reliability requirements; investigators qualified elsewhere in the EEA or Switzerland must have their qualification recognised by Justis. Investigators hold no police powers, and surveillance and recording remain subject to the GDPR, the UAVG and criminal law.

      Corporate & public records

      The KVK Business Register records Dutch companies, foundations, associations and branches. Paid KVK extracts show registration details, officers and authorised representatives, and the register also supplies deposited documents, corporate history and filed financial statements, whose level of detail depends on company size. The KVK also maintains the UBO register under anti-money laundering rules. After the Court of Justice of the European Union ruled in November 2022 that general public access to beneficial ownership registers was incompatible with the EU Charter, public access to UBO data was suspended; access is now limited to authorities and specified parties. Land registry records are held by the Kadaster.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery of public officials, including foreign officials, and private sector bribery are offences under the Dutch Criminal Code, and legal entities can be prosecuted directly. The Money Laundering and Terrorist Financing (Prevention) Act (Wwft) imposes customer due diligence and reporting duties, with unusual transactions reported to FIU-Nederland. The Public Prosecution Service (Openbaar Ministerie) prosecutes, with the Fiscal Information and Investigation Service (FIOD) leading complex fraud and corruption investigations.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Netherlands

      TDSC first establishes whether a matter involves Dutch employees, Dutch data subjects or evidence intended for Dutch courts, since each brings different obligations under the UAVG and employment law. Desk research draws on KVK extracts and deposited accounts, published judgments, Kadaster records where relevant, media archives and reputable commercial databases. Beneficial ownership information is sought only through channels open to a qualifying party, normally via Dutch counsel. Any investigative activity carried out in the Netherlands, such as interviews, observation or local enquiries, is coordinated with a licensed Dutch agency working under its privacy code. Personal data is minimised and documented against a GDPR lawful basis, and transfers to Nashville rely on a recognised mechanism such as the EU-US Data Privacy Framework or Standard Contractual Clauses.

      TDSC has no office in Netherlands. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Netherlands: Frequently Asked Questions

      Are private investigation agencies licensed in the Netherlands?

      Yes. Agencies require a licence under the Wpbr, granted on behalf of the Minister of Justice and Security, and investigators must meet qualification and reliability requirements. Investigators qualified in another EEA country or Switzerland must have their qualification recognised by Justis. TDSC does not hold a Dutch licence and coordinates with licensed agencies for work in the Netherlands.

      Can we still look up the ultimate owners of a Dutch company?

      Not through public access. Following the CJEU ruling of November 2022, public access to the Dutch UBO register was suspended and is now limited to authorities and specified parties. Ownership is usually reconstructed from KVK filings, deposited accounts, court records and other lawful sources, with a formal request made through counsel where a party qualifies.

      What does a KVK extract show?

      A KVK extract shows the registered details of a company, including its name, registration number, address, legal form, officers and authorised representatives. Deposited financial statements and historical information can be ordered separately. The detail of filed accounts depends on company size, so smaller companies often disclose limited financial information.

      Can we use English-language proceedings in the Netherlands?

      Yes, before the Netherlands Commercial Court, which has operated within the Amsterdam courts since 1 January 2019 and conducts proceedings and issues judgments in English, with an NCC Court of Appeal. Other Dutch courts generally work in Dutch, so evidence prepared for them may need certified translation.

      How does Dutch law treat information about criminal offences?

      Strictly. Under the GDPR and the UAVG, data on criminal convictions and offences may be processed only within defined exceptions, such as the establishment or defence of legal claims, and the Dutch definition extends to court-imposed prohibitions. Investigative reports involving such data are scoped and documented with this in mind.
