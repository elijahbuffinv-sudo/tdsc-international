      Afghanistan

      Investigations Touching Afghanistan

      Matters touching Afghanistan are shaped by de facto Taliban administration since August 2021, an opaque legal order and US terrorism designations. TDSC scopes lawful, sanctions-screened due diligence, counterparty and supply-chain review, and risk advisory for clients outside the country, working from Nashville.

      Overview

      Afghanistan at a Glance

      Since the Taliban regained control of Kabul on 15 August 2021, Afghanistan has been governed by a de facto administration that styles itself the Islamic Emirate. The 2004 Constitution is treated as suspended, the National Assembly was dissolved, and major rules are issued as decrees and ministerial directives. Republic-era laws have not been formally repealed, but courts now apply Hanafi jurisprudence with judges drawn largely from religious scholars, and practice varies between provinces. Russia became the first state to formally recognise the administration in July 2025; most governments have not. For investigators this means fragile institutions, records whose completeness and integrity cannot be assumed, and a security environment that limits what can be done on the ground.

      Organisations commission work touching Afghanistan chiefly to manage exposure rather than to pursue opportunity. Humanitarian and development organisations need partner and vendor vetting that addresses diversion and designated-person risk. Banks, payment firms and exporters in neighbouring markets review counterparties with Afghan links. Investors and traders assess the mining contracts the administration has awarded for copper, gold, iron and other minerals, many with partners from China, Iran and Turkey. Law firms handle asset tracing connected to the former government period, immigration and resettlement matters requiring document verification, and disputes involving Afghan-owned businesses operating from the Gulf, Pakistan, Central Asia and Europe.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Afghanistan Concentrates

      01

      Humanitarian partner and vendor vetting

      Aid agencies and donors screen implementing partners, suppliers and money service providers for links to designated persons, diversion and conflicts of interest. TDSC combines registry checks, adverse media in Dari, Pashto and English, and sanctions screening, documenting findings so they can support the client's own compliance obligations.

      02

      Mining and resource counterparty review

      Mining contracts awarded since 2021, often through Afghan companies with Chinese, Iranian or Turkish partners, carry ownership, transparency and enforceability questions. TDSC maps the counterparties, their foreign affiliates and their exposure to sanctioned persons before clients outside Afghanistan trade, finance or insure related activity.

      03

      Regional trade and payment exposure

      Banks and traders in Pakistan, Iran, Central Asia and the Gulf face Afghan-linked counterparties and informal value transfer networks. TDSC reviews corporate footprints, trade routes and beneficial ownership indicators so compliance teams can apply enhanced due diligence with evidence rather than assumption.

      04

      Asset tracing from the former republic

      Litigation and recovery matters sometimes concern assets moved abroad by former officials, contractors or their associates before or after 2021. TDSC focuses on jurisdictions where assets landed, using property, corporate and court records in those countries rather than Afghan sources of uncertain integrity.

      05

      Identity and document verification

      Immigration, resettlement, employment and insurance matters often turn on Afghan educational, civil or employment documents that cannot be confirmed through functioning institutions. TDSC assesses internal consistency, corroborating records held abroad and open sources, and reports clearly on what can and cannot be verified.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Afghanistan

      Legal system

      Afghanistan's legal order is in transition and not fully documented. The de facto authorities treat Hanafi Sunni jurisprudence as the supreme source of law, govern by decree of the Amir and ministerial directive, and have not adopted a new constitution. Republic-era courts, including the specialised anti-corruption court, closed after 2021, and judges were replaced largely by clerics. A Commercial Arbitration Law was enacted in 2007, and the Afghanistan Center for Commercial Dispute Resolution was established in 2015 under the chamber of commerce; we have not confirmed how either operates today.

      Data protection

      No comprehensive data protection law identified · Regulator: No dedicated data protection authority identified

      We have not identified a comprehensive data protection statute or an independent supervisory authority in Afghanistan, and an ITU country profile recorded no officially recognised data protection regulation. That absence does not make personal data unprotected for TDSC's purposes. Information about Afghan nationals is frequently processed by clients in the European Union, United Kingdom or United States, whose own laws apply to collection, retention and transfer. Given documented risks to former officials, journalists, women professionals and minority communities, TDSC treats data on Afghan individuals as high-sensitivity, minimises collection and restricts onward sharing regardless of local law.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified a current licensing regime for private investigators in Afghanistan. Republic-era rules governed private security companies through the Ministry of Interior, and the security sector was later folded into a state protection force, but none of that addresses investigative consulting, and the de facto authorities' position is not publicly documented. In practice, independent fieldwork carries serious legal and physical risk to anyone asking questions locally, particularly about officials, security forces or women. TDSC does not conduct covert field enquiries in Afghanistan and relies on lawful remote research and vetted professionals outside the country.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies are registered with the Afghanistan Central Business Registry (ACBR), historically operated under the Ministry of Commerce and Industry as a one-stop registry that issues business licences and tax identification numbers. An online verification service has allowed searches by registration, licence or TIN number, returning limited identifying data rather than full filings. Most private companies are formed as limited liability companies, and licence records have recorded officers' names. We have not identified a public beneficial ownership register. Land and court records are largely paper-based and their reliability after 2021 should not be assumed.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Under the former republic, Afghanistan adopted an Anti-Money Laundering and Proceeds of Crime Law, a revised version of which replaced the original law published in 2004, alongside a terrorist-financing law, with FinTRACA, the financial intelligence unit housed at Da Afghanistan Bank, as the reporting centre. The Egmont Group suspended FinTRACA's access to its information-sharing platform in August 2021. The specialised anti-corruption courts and the former anti-corruption bodies are no longer functioning, and current enforcement practice is not publicly documented.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Afghanistan

      Every matter touching Afghanistan begins with sanctions screening. The Taliban is designated by the United States as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist and appears on the SDN List; TDSC, as a US company, does not accept engagements that would involve prohibited dealings with designated persons or violate US or other applicable sanctions, and does not suggest workarounds. Most engagements are therefore lawful due diligence and risk advisory for clients outside Afghanistan. Research relies on open sources, registry extracts, foreign corporate and court records, and media in Dari, Pashto and English. TDSC does not send personnel into the country or task sources in ways that could endanger them, involves the client's counsel on sanctions questions, and holds data on Afghan individuals under strict access controls.

      TDSC has no office in Afghanistan. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Afghanistan: Frequently Asked Questions

      Is Afghanistan under comprehensive US sanctions?

      According to OFAC guidance, Afghanistan is not subject to a comprehensive embargo of the kind applied to Cuba or Iran. However, the Taliban is designated as a Specially Designated Global Terrorist, so dealings with the group and designated individuals are generally prohibited for US persons, subject to general licences. TDSC screens every Afghan-related matter and expects clients to take sanctions advice from their own counsel.

      Can company information in Afghanistan be checked online?

      The Afghanistan Central Business Registry has offered an online verification service that confirms basic identifiers such as registration, licence and tax numbers. It does not provide the depth of filings available in many registries, and post-2021 data quality is uncertain. TDSC treats a registry match as a starting point and corroborates it with trade data, foreign filings and media sources.

      Does TDSC carry out fieldwork inside Afghanistan?

      No. TDSC does not deploy staff to Afghanistan or commission covert local enquiries. Asking questions about individuals, officials or businesses can expose local people to serious harm, and the legal framework for such work is unclear. Engagements are designed around remote, lawful research and, where needed, professionals operating in neighbouring or third countries.

      How reliable are Afghan documents for immigration or employment checks?

      Reliability varies. Documents issued under the former republic may be confirmable through archived data, former employers or foreign contractors, while documents issued since 2021 are harder to verify independently. TDSC reports on consistency, corroboration and gaps, and does not claim access to government databases. Final decisions rest with the client and its advisers.

      Which laws govern commercial disputes with Afghan counterparties?

      This is genuinely uncertain. Republic-era statutes, including a 2007 commercial arbitration law, have not been formally repealed, but courts now apply Hanafi jurisprudence and decrees, and enforcement of foreign judgments or awards is unpredictable. Clients usually structure contracts and disputes outside Afghanistan. TDSC supports counsel with facts on counterparties and assets but does not give legal advice.
