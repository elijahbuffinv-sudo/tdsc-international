      Overview

      Hong Kong at a Glance

      Hong Kong is a Special Administrative Region of China with its own common law legal system, courts, currency and company registry. Its registries, bilingual court system and long tradition of English-language commercial practice make it one of the more workable jurisdictions in the region for documentary research. Personal data is governed by the Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance, which since 2021 has included criminal offences for doxxing. The national security framework, including the 2020 National Security Law and the Safeguarding National Security Ordinance in force since 23 March 2024 with its offences concerning state secrets and external interference, is an essential consideration when scoping any inquiry with a political or governmental dimension.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving Hong Kong because a large share of mainland Chinese and regional business is owned, financed or invoiced through Hong Kong companies. Typical matters include tracing ownership behind holding companies and trading entities, verifying counterparties in trade finance and supply chains, investigating fraud and misappropriation routed through Hong Kong bank accounts, and locating assets for enforcement of judgments and arbitral awards, including awards from the Hong Kong International Arbitration Centre. Listed companies, family offices and financial institutions also commission background research on directors, investors and transaction counterparties before deals and appointments.

      Law & Regulation

      What applies before any work touching Hong Kong begins

      Legal system

      Hong Kong retains a common law system under the Basic Law, separate from the mainland's civil law system. Civil matters of any value can be heard in the Court of First Instance of the High Court, which deals routinely with company winding-up, bankruptcy, construction and arbitration matters. Appeals go to the Court of Appeal and, with leave, to the Court of Final Appeal. Chinese and English are both official court languages. The Hong Kong International Arbitration Centre, established in 1985, is a leading regional arbitral institution and the default appointing authority under the Arbitration Ordinance (Cap. 609).

      Data protection

      Personal Data (Privacy) Ordinance (Cap. 486, 1996; amended 2012 and 2021) · Regulator: Office of the Privacy Commissioner for Personal Data (PCPD)

      The PDPO requires personal data to be collected lawfully and fairly for a lawful purpose, kept adequate and not excessive, used only for the notified purpose unless an exemption applies or the data subject consents, kept secure and retained no longer than necessary. Data subjects must generally be informed at collection. Section 33, which would restrict transfers outside Hong Kong, has not been brought into force, so there is no statutory transfer restriction, though PCPD guidance recommends model contract clauses. Since October 2021, disclosing personal data without consent with intent or recklessness as to harm is a criminal offence, a point of direct relevance to investigative reporting.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified a statutory licensing regime specific to private investigators in Hong Kong, and investigative firms generally operate as ordinary businesses. Their work is nonetheless constrained by the PDPO, including its doxxing offences carrying up to five years' imprisonment where harm results, by general criminal law on trespass, harassment and access to computer systems, and by the national security legislation, which criminalises unlawful acquisition, possession and disclosure of state secrets and applies to organisations doing business in Hong Kong. Investigators have no special powers or access to non-public government records. Covert recording and surveillance carry legal and evidential risk and should be assessed with Hong Kong counsel first.

      Corporate & public records

      The Companies Registry maintains public records of Hong Kong companies, searchable online through its Integrated Companies Registry Information System, including incorporation documents, annual returns, registered office and director and company secretary details. Recent reforms protect directors' residential addresses and full identification numbers from general public inspection. Since 1 March 2018, Hong Kong companies must keep a Significant Controllers Register of beneficial owners, which is available for inspection by law enforcement officers on demand rather than by the public. Land ownership records are maintained by the Land Registry, and judgments are published by the Judiciary.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      The Prevention of Bribery Ordinance (Cap. 201) prohibits bribery of public servants and, under section 9, bribery in the private sector, where an agent may not solicit or accept an advantage without the principal's permission. It is enforced by the Independent Commission Against Corruption, whose powers derive from the ICAC Ordinance (Cap. 204). Money laundering and the failure to report knowledge or suspicion of crime proceeds are offences, with suspicious transaction reports made to the Joint Financial Intelligence Unit.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Hong Kong

      TDSC scopes Hong Kong matters by first confirming the purpose of the inquiry and the lawful use of any personal data, mindful of the PDPO's notification and purpose principles and its doxxing offences. We also assess at the outset whether the subject touches government bodies, state-owned enterprises or politically sensitive topics, and decline work that could involve state secrets or national security concerns. Research from Nashville uses Companies Registry filings, land and court records, published judgments, regulatory announcements and open sources in English and Chinese. Where interviews, site visits or court-related steps are needed, TDSC coordinates with Hong Kong solicitors and qualified local professionals. Where related mainland entities are involved, mainland work is scoped separately under PRC rules.

      TDSC has no office in Hong Kong. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Hong Kong Concentrates

      01

      Ownership behind holding companies

      Tracing who stands behind Hong Kong holding and trading companies used in mainland and regional structures, combining Companies Registry filings, annual returns, mainland and offshore records and open sources, while recognising that the Significant Controllers Register is not publicly searchable and that nominee arrangements are common.

      02

      Trade and supply chain verification

      Verifying Hong Kong counterparties in trade finance, sourcing and logistics, including whether a company has genuine operations, staff and premises or acts only as an invoicing vehicle, and identifying adverse litigation, winding-up petitions and links to sanctioned or high-risk parties.

      03

      Fraud and misappropriation tracing

      Supporting counsel in matters where funds are diverted through Hong Kong companies and accounts, reconstructing corporate relationships from public records to support applications by Hong Kong solicitors for disclosure, injunctive relief or proprietary remedies in the Court of First Instance.

      04

      Award and judgment enforcement

      Identifying the Hong Kong companies, real property and other publicly recorded interests of a debtor to support enforcement of arbitral awards and foreign judgments, including awards seated in Hong Kong under the HKIAC rules and enforcement strategies involving related mainland entities.

      05

      Director and investor background research

      Lawful background research on proposed directors, investors and senior hires for listed companies, family offices and financial institutions, covering directorships, litigation, insolvency, regulatory actions and media, with reporting designed to meet PDPO requirements on purpose and data minimisation.

      Questions

      Hong Kong: Frequently Asked Questions

      Are private investigators licensed in Hong Kong?

      We have not identified a dedicated licensing statute for private investigators in Hong Kong. That does not mean the field is unregulated in practice: the PDPO, its doxxing offences, general criminal law and national security legislation all limit what investigators may do. TDSC confirms the legal position for each matter with Hong Kong counsel before any local fieldwork is commissioned.

      Can the beneficial owners of a Hong Kong company be found in a public register?

      No. Hong Kong companies have kept a Significant Controllers Register since 1 March 2018, but it is open to law enforcement officers on demand, not to the public. Ownership is usually traced through shareholder details in annual returns, linked companies, overseas records and open sources, and TDSC reports clearly where ownership cannot be confirmed.

      How does the Safeguarding National Security Ordinance affect due diligence?

      The ordinance, in force since 23 March 2024, created offences relating to state secrets and external interference, some applying to organisations doing business in Hong Kong. Ordinary commercial due diligence on private companies is generally unaffected, but inquiries touching government, state enterprises or political matters need careful scoping, and TDSC seeks local legal advice before proceeding.

      Can investigation results containing personal data be sent from Hong Kong to the United States?

      The PDPO's cross-border transfer provision, section 33, has not been brought into force, so there is no statutory prohibition. The data protection principles still apply, and the PCPD recommends contractual safeguards. TDSC limits personal data in reports to what the stated purpose requires and agrees handling and retention terms with the client.

      Can TDSC help enforce an award or judgment against a Hong Kong company?

      TDSC supports counsel by identifying publicly recorded assets and corporate relationships of the debtor, such as subsidiaries, land holdings and connected companies, and by monitoring for asset movements visible in public filings. Enforcement steps themselves, including applications to the Court of First Instance, are taken by Hong Kong solicitors and counsel.
