      Overview

      Dominican Republic at a Glance

      The Dominican Republic is the largest economy in the Caribbean and Central America and has about 11.5 million people. Its legal system follows the civil law tradition, with codes that originated in France. Dominican courts often look to French case law where the texts are the same. Companies are governed by Law 479-08 on Commercial Companies and are recorded in the Registro Mercantil, which regional chambers of commerce maintain under Law 3-02. Investors often report that court proceedings are slow. Personal data is regulated by Law 172-13, a consent-based law with no dedicated supervisory authority, so how information is gathered and transferred needs careful planning.

      Organisations commission work involving the Dominican Republic because of its large foreign direct investment in tourism, real estate, telecommunications, mining and free trade zones. As of early 2024, the free zone regime under Law 8-90 had more than 800 companies in 84 parks producing medical devices, apparel, tobacco and electronics for export. Typical needs include checking hotel and real estate partners, researching suppliers in free zones, reviewing exposure to public procurement and state-owned enterprises, tracing assets of judgment debtors, and investigating fraud with links to the large Dominican diaspora in the United States and Spain.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Dominican Republic

      Legal system

      The Dominican Republic has a civil law system built on codes of French origin, including the Civil Code, and on special statutes such as Law 479-08 on commercial companies. The Supreme Court of Justice heads the ordinary courts, above the courts of appeal and the courts of first instance, which sit in chambers. A Constitutional Court was created under the 2010 Constitution. Commercial arbitration is governed by Law 489-08. The Santo Domingo Chamber of Commerce runs an arbitration centre, the Corte de Arbitraje y Resolución Alternativa de Conflictos, which was created under Law 181-09.

      Data protection

      Law No. 172-13 on the Comprehensive Protection of Personal Data (2013) · Regulator: No general data protection authority; Superintendency of Banks for credit information bureaus; Pro Consumidor for consumer matters

      Law 172-13 generally requires the data subject's consent to process personal data. Exceptions include data from public sources and processing needed to perform a contract or meet a legal obligation. There is no general data protection authority for private-sector processing. Individuals enforce their rights mainly through habeas data actions in the courts, and the Superintendency of Banks oversees credit bureaus. International transfers generally require the data subject's express authorisation, or must be necessary to perform a contract with them. In practice, investigators should rely on public sources and documented legal bases. Reforms to modernise the law have been discussed for several years.

      Private investigators

      Private detective agencies have historically been supervised by the Superintendencia de Vigilancia y Seguridad Privada, which was created by Decree 1128-03 and sits within the armed forces ministry. A press report from 2010 described the oversight as resting on a decree and regulation rather than a dedicated statute. In 2019 the Senate approved, in first reading, a bill to regulate private security and private detectives. We have not confirmed whether that bill, or any successor, has been enacted. Interception of communications and unauthorised access to systems are criminal offences under Law 53-07 on high technology crimes. Local investigative work should be done by appropriately authorised professionals.

      Corporate & public records

      Under Law 3-02, companies register in the Registro Mercantil kept by the chamber of commerce for their region. The Santo Domingo Chamber of Commerce runs the largest registry and provides an online search portal. Registry certifications generally show the company's constitution, registered address, administrators and, for some company types, partners or shareholders. Certified copies can be ordered. Since Law 155-17, entities and obliged parties must identify their beneficial owners, and the tax authority (DGII) plays a role in collecting that information. We have not confirmed that beneficial ownership information is public. Real property is registered through the Jurisdicción Inmobiliaria under a Torrens-style title system.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Law 155-17 against money laundering and terrorist financing sets AML duties and the requirement to identify beneficial owners. The Unidad de Análisis Financiero (UAF), attached to the Ministry of Finance, receives suspicious transaction reports. The DGII supervises designated non-financial businesses. Law 340-06 on public procurement addresses conflicts of interest in public contracting. Corruption cases are prosecuted by the Procuraduría Especializada de Persecución de la Corrupción Administrativa (PEPCA) within the Public Ministry. The country has ratified the UN Convention against Corruption but is not a party to the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Dominican Republic

      TDSC handles Dominican Republic matters from Nashville. We first map which registries actually hold the records that matter: the regional chamber of commerce for company records, the Jurisdicción Inmobiliaria for land, the courts for litigation, and records in the United States or Spain where diaspora links exist. Because Law 172-13 is built on consent, we document the legal basis for any collection of personal data, prefer public sources, and keep cross-border transfers of personal data to what the engagement needs. Where certified copies, field verification or interviews are required, we coordinate appropriately authorised local professionals and work with the client's Dominican counsel on legal questions, especially on admissibility and on whether a bill regulating detectives has come into force.

      TDSC has no office in Dominican Republic. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Dominican Republic Concentrates

      01

      Tourism and real estate partners

      Background and litigation research on hotel operators, developers and land sellers in markets such as Punta Cana, Cap Cana and the planned Pedernales tourism zone. This includes checking title chains through the Jurisdicción Inmobiliaria and looking for prior project failures or disputes.

      02

      Free zone supplier verification

      Checking the corporate existence, ownership, reputation and operating footprint of manufacturers in free zones regulated under Law 8-90 for medical devices, apparel, tobacco and electronics. This supports supply chain, customs and forced-labour compliance reviews.

      03

      Public procurement and SOE exposure

      Integrity due diligence for companies bidding on public contracts or partnering with state-owned enterprises in electricity, banking and refining. The work focuses on politically exposed persons, conflicts of interest and past procurement controversies that could matter under the FCPA or UK Bribery Act.

      04

      Diaspora-linked fraud and assets

      Tracing funds and property where fraud, investment schemes or disputed estates connect the United States, Spain and the Dominican Republic. Dominican registry and court findings are combined with records in the counterparty's other jurisdictions.

      05

      Mining and energy counterparties

      Reputational and ownership research on contractors, landholders and local partners around mining operations such as Pueblo Viejo and around energy generation projects, where community, environmental and permitting disputes can affect investment value.

      Questions

      Dominican Republic: Frequently Asked Questions

      Where are Dominican company records held?

      Each company is registered with the Registro Mercantil of the chamber of commerce for its region under Law 3-02. Santo Domingo holds the largest share. Certifications can confirm incorporation, registered address and administrators, and certified copies of filings can be ordered. Records of companies registered outside Santo Domingo may need a separate search at another chamber.

      Can we see who really owns a Dominican company?

      Law 155-17 requires beneficial owners to be identified and gives the tax authority a role in collecting that information, but we have not confirmed public access to it. In practice we combine registry filings, shareholder records where they are available, litigation, property and media sources, and relevant records abroad. We tell the client how confident we are in any ownership conclusion.

      Does Law 172-13 restrict background research on individuals?

      Yes, it can. The law is built on consent, with exceptions for public sources and for processing needed to meet contractual or legal obligations. Background research should therefore rely on public records and a clearly documented purpose. Transfers of personal data abroad generally require the data subject's express authorisation or contractual necessity, so we keep reporting focused and collect only what the stated purpose needs.

      Are private detectives licensed in the Dominican Republic?

      Oversight has historically sat with the Superintendencia de Vigilancia y Seguridad Privada under Decree 1128-03, and a bill to put private security and detectives on a statutory footing passed the Senate in first reading in 2019. Because the current status is uncertain, we confirm the authorisation of any local professional with Dominican counsel before work starts.

      How are commercial disputes typically resolved?

      Many commercial contracts provide for arbitration under Law 489-08, either ad hoc or through the arbitration centre of the Santo Domingo Chamber of Commerce. Court litigation runs through the courts of first instance, the courts of appeal and the Supreme Court of Justice, and investors often describe it as slow. Our research supports counsel with facts, evidence and asset information. We do not provide legal advice.
