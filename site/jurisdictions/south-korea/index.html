      South Korea: Investigative Consulting

      Investigative consulting for matters touching South Korea means working within one of the world's stricter personal information regimes, reading Korean-language court and disclosure records, and understanding how conglomerate group structures, related-party dealings and supplier networks shape commercial risk for foreign counterparties.

      Overview

      South Korea at a Glance

      South Korea is a civil law jurisdiction with a well-documented corporate disclosure culture and an exacting approach to personal data. Listed companies and larger unlisted firms file detailed reports through the Financial Supervisory Service's DART system, and court-maintained commercial registers record directors, auditors and capital. At the same time, the Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) and the Credit Information Use and Protection Act sharply limit what can be collected about individuals, and Korea has no national licence for private detectives. Investigative work that would be routine elsewhere, such as locating a person or researching private life, is legally constrained, so scoping and method selection matter from the outset.

      Organisations commission work involving South Korea because of its weight in semiconductors, batteries, shipbuilding, automotive, steel, petrochemicals and consumer electronics, and its role as a supplier and investor in the United States. The US-Korea Free Trade Agreement (KORUS), revised with effect from 1 January 2019, keeps bilateral trade volumes high. Typical exposures include counterparty and supplier verification, the influence of large business groups (chaebol) on related-party transactions, trade secret and technology leakage disputes, distributor and joint-venture conflicts, and pre-acquisition due diligence on Korean targets or Korean investors entering US markets.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving South Korea Concentrates

      01

      Supplier and counterparty verification

      Confirming that Korean manufacturers, trading companies and component suppliers are what they claim to be, using court registry extracts, DART filings, litigation records and trade data, with attention to group affiliation, related-party sales and the financial position of mid-tier suppliers in semiconductor, battery and automotive chains.

      02

      Chaebol and group structure analysis

      Mapping affiliate relationships, cross-holdings and intra-group transactions within large business groups using Fair Trade Commission disclosures and DART reports, to assess whether a counterparty's apparent independence, pricing or credit support depends on decisions taken elsewhere in the group.

      03

      Trade secret and technology leakage

      Supporting counsel in matters where engineers, data or process know-how move between competitors, particularly in semiconductors, displays and batteries, by assembling open-source and documentary chronologies while leaving forensic imaging, interviews and evidence preservation to Korean counsel and court-supervised procedures.

      04

      Pre-transaction due diligence

      Background research on Korean targets, founders and investors before acquisitions, minority investments or US market entry, covering registry history, litigation, regulatory sanctions, media coverage in Korean and reputational issues, scoped to stay within PIPA limits on personal information about individuals.

      05

      Distributor and joint-venture disputes

      Assembling documented facts on a Korean partner's corporate changes, asset movements, new affiliates and competing business lines where a distribution, licensing or joint-venture relationship has broken down, to inform US or Korean counsel on claims, injunctive relief and the practicality of enforcement.

      Law & Regulation

      What applies before any work touching South Korea begins

      Legal system

      South Korea has a civil law system built on codified statutes, including the Civil Act and the Commercial Act. Commercial disputes are heard by district courts at first instance, with appeals to the high courts and the Supreme Court; specialised patent, family and administrative courts sit alongside them, and the Seoul Bankruptcy Court handles major insolvency and rehabilitation cases nationwide. The Korean Commercial Arbitration Board (KCAB), with an international branch, administers arbitration under the Arbitration Act. Proceedings are conducted in Korean.

      Data protection

      Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA), as amended, with sectoral rules under the Credit Information Use and Protection Act and the Act on the Protection and Use of Location Information · Regulator: Personal Information Protection Commission (PIPC)

      PIPA generally requires a lawful basis, most often consent, for collecting and using personal information, and Korean practice favours explicit, itemised notices. Overseas transfers generally require consent unless an exception applies, such as disclosed outsourcing necessary to perform a contract, recipient certification measures recognised by the PIPC, or an adequacy recognition; the PIPC recognised the EU as adequate in September 2025. Amendments passed in March 2026, mostly effective from September 2026, raise administrative penalties and tighten breach notification. Credit and location data carry separate restrictions, and retail credit data must be stored locally, according to the US State Department.

      Private investigators

      Korea has no national licensing statute for private detectives; only private-sector certifications exist. The Credit Information Use and Protection Act has long restricted detective-type business, including locating specific individuals and investigating private life, except where permitted by law. Restrictions on using the title 'detective' (tamjeong) were relaxed by a 2020 amendment, but the underlying prohibitions on tracing individuals and researching private affairs remain significant. Covert recording of conversations to which one is not a party, unauthorised location tracking and collection of personal data without a lawful basis can attract criminal liability. Evidence-gathering in disputes is therefore generally channelled through Korean counsel and court procedures.

      Corporate & public records

      Commercial registers are maintained by the court registry system and are searchable through the Supreme Court's Internet Registry Office (IROS), which issues extracts showing a company's name, registered office, purpose, capital, representative director, directors and statutory auditors; shareholders of stock companies are generally not shown. Real estate registers are also available through the same system. The Financial Supervisory Service's DART system publishes annual and quarterly reports, audit reports, large shareholding reports and Fair Trade Commission group disclosures. We have not identified a public beneficial ownership register; beneficial ownership information is collected by financial institutions during customer due diligence.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Principal laws include the Improper Solicitation and Graft Act (the 'Kim Young-ran Act', in force since 28 September 2016), the Act on the Prevention of Conflict of Interest (in force since May 2022), the Anti-Corruption and Civil Rights Commission Act (2008), the Act on Combating Bribery of Foreign Public Officials in International Business Transactions and the Criminal Act. The Anti-Corruption and Civil Rights Commission (ACRC), the prosecution service, the Board of Audit and Inspection and the Korea Financial Intelligence Unit (KoFIU) are the main bodies.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching South Korea

      TDSC scopes South Korea matters from Nashville and begins by testing each proposed task against PIPA and the Credit Information Use and Protection Act, because methods such as locating individuals or researching private life that may be lawful elsewhere can be prohibited in Korea. Research concentrates on lawful public sources: court registry extracts, DART and Fair Trade Commission disclosures, published judgments, Korean-language media and trade data, read by Korean-literate researchers. Where a matter needs work on the ground, such as site visits or document retrieval, TDSC coordinates qualified local professionals and works alongside the client's Korean counsel, who direct evidence-gathering for litigation. Personal information is minimised, held in the United States under documented controls and transferred only on a recognised legal basis.

      TDSC has no office in South Korea. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      South Korea: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can a private investigator locate a person in South Korea?

      Locating specific individuals or investigating their private life is restricted under the Credit Information Use and Protection Act, and Korea has no national detective licence. Address and resident registration data are not open to private searchers. Where a person must be found for litigation or enforcement, Korean counsel generally uses court procedures. TDSC limits its own work to lawful public sources and does not attempt tracing that Korean law prohibits.

      What can be learned about a Korean company from public records?

      Court registry extracts available through the Internet Registry Office show the company's representative director, directors, statutory auditors, capital and registered office. Listed and larger unlisted companies file reports on DART, including audited financial statements and major shareholder disclosures. Shareholders of unlisted stock companies are usually not on the public register, so ownership often has to be inferred from filings or confirmed contractually.

      Does PIPA affect a US company's internal investigation involving Korean employees?

      Yes. PIPA applies to personal information processed about employees in Korea, and Korean practice expects clear notice and, often, consent for specific uses. Transferring employee data to the United States generally needs consent or another recognised basis. Employers usually involve Korean employment and data protection counsel before collecting devices, emails or interview material, and TDSC scopes any support it provides around that advice.

      Are Korean court judgments and filings available to researchers?

      Korean courts publish a selection of judgments, usually with personal identifiers removed, and case progress can be checked with a case number. Full case files are not generally open to third parties. Research therefore relies on published decisions, regulatory sanctions, DART-disclosed litigation and Korean-language press reporting, with counsel obtaining further records through party access where a client is involved in the proceedings.

      How does TDSC handle language and translation for Korean matters?

      Korean-language sources, including registry extracts, DART filings, judgments and media, are read in the original by Korean-literate researchers. Key documents relied on in a report are identified with their original titles and dates, and certified translation is arranged through qualified translators where a document is to be used in US proceedings or filed with a court.
