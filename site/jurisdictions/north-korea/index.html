      Overview

      North Korea at a Glance

      The Democratic People's Republic of Korea is a closed, state-directed economy with no independent judiciary, no public company registry and no open media. Information about North Korean entities comes almost entirely from outside the country: United States and United Nations sanctions designations, government advisories, court filings in third countries, shipping and trade data, and specialist research. The country is subject to extensive United States sanctions, codified in the North Korea Sanctions Regulations (31 CFR Part 510), and to United Nations Security Council measures. The UN Panel of Experts that monitored those measures ceased operating after its mandate renewal was vetoed in March 2024, removing one of the more detailed public sources on evasion.

      Organisations outside North Korea commission investigative work because North Korean networks seek access to foreign revenue, goods and technology through intermediaries. Recurring exposures include remote IT workers using false identities to obtain contracts with foreign companies, front companies in third countries engaged in procurement or shipping, ship-to-ship transfers and vessel identity manipulation, and theft of cryptocurrency. Companies, platforms, financial institutions, shipping and insurance firms and their counsel seek help understanding whether a counterparty, contractor or transaction may have a concealed North Korean nexus, and how to document the reasoning behind their own decisions.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving North Korea Concentrates

      01

      Remote IT worker screening

      Review of contractors and remote employees whose identities, locations or payment details show the inconsistencies described in the May 2022 joint US advisory, such as shifting IP addresses, mismatched biographies, laptop forwarding requests and payments routed to third-country accounts.

      02

      Front company and procurement review

      Research into suppliers, buyers and freight forwarders in third countries whose ownership, addresses or trading patterns overlap with designated North Korean networks, to inform a client's own compliance decision on whether to proceed.

      03

      Maritime and shipping exposure

      Review of vessels, owners, managers and charter counterparties for indicators associated with North Korean sanctions evasion, including ownership changes, flag hopping, identity manipulation and ship-to-ship transfer patterns documented in public sources.

      04

      Cryptocurrency incident context

      Support for counsel and compliance teams assessing whether a theft or suspicious flow has been publicly attributed to North Korean actors, compiling government attributions and open research, and coordinating with qualified blockchain analysts where needed.

      05

      Historic exposure lookbacks

      Structured review of past counterparties and transactions after a red flag emerges, to help counsel assess scope before any voluntary disclosure decision. TDSC provides factual research only; legal conclusions remain with the client's sanctions counsel.

      Law & Regulation

      What applies before any work touching North Korea begins

      Legal system

      North Korea has a codified civil law system with origins in the Japanese colonial period and strong Soviet-era influence, operating under the direction of the Workers' Party of Korea. The court system has three tiers, with the Central Court at the top and provincial and county courts below, and the Central Public Prosecutor's Office supervising legal affairs. There is no independent commercial court. The state has enacted foreign investment and special economic zone legislation, including for the Rason zone, but these provide no meaningful avenue for independent investigation or dispute resolution.

      Data protection

      No comprehensive data protection law identified · Regulator: None identified

      We have not identified a comprehensive personal data protection law or independent regulator in North Korea. This has little practical bearing on investigative work, because no lawful enquiries are made inside the country. What matters is the data protection law of the jurisdictions where research is carried out and where subjects are located. Work on suspected North Korean IT workers, for example, typically involves personal data of applicants and contractors in the United States, Europe or Asia, and is handled under those jurisdictions' rules, with collection limited to what the client's compliance question requires.

      Private investigators

      There is no lawful private investigation sector in North Korea, and we have not identified any framework under which private investigators could operate. TDSC does not conduct, commission or coordinate any enquiries, source contacts or fieldwork inside the country, and does not engage with North Korean persons or entities. All work concerning North Korea is carried out from outside it, using lawful public sources, government publications, sanctions lists, court records and commercial data in other jurisdictions, and is subject to sanctions screening before it begins.

      Corporate & public records

      There is no publicly accessible company registry, beneficial ownership register, court record system or land registry in North Korea. Information on North Korean state entities, banks, trading companies and vessels is drawn from United States Treasury and State Department designations and advisories, United Nations Security Council materials including past Panel of Experts reports, court and enforcement records in third countries, ship registries, and corporate registries where front or associated companies are incorporated, such as in China, Hong Kong, Southeast Asia or elsewhere.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      North Korea has no independent anti-corruption or financial intelligence framework capable of external verification. For investigative purposes, the relevant controls are those applied by other jurisdictions: United States sanctions under the North Korean Sanctions and Policy Enhancement Act of 2016 and related executive orders, United Nations Security Council resolutions, and the anti-money laundering and sanctions regimes of the countries where intermediaries operate.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching North Korea

      TDSC, as a US company, screens every matter touching North Korea for sanctions compliance before acceptance and does not accept engagements that would violate US or other applicable sanctions. Work is limited to lawful due diligence and risk advisory for clients outside North Korea: for example, assessing whether a contractor, supplier, vessel or counterparty shows indicators of a concealed North Korean connection. TDSC conducts no enquiries in the country, has no contact with North Korean persons or entities, and does not facilitate payments, travel, communications or trade. Research relies on government designations and advisories, UN materials, third-country corporate and court records and commercial data. Where findings suggest a possible violation, TDSC reports them to the client so that its sanctions counsel can advise on next steps.

      TDSC has no office in North Korea. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      North Korea: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can TDSC investigate a person or company inside North Korea?

      No. TDSC does not make enquiries, use sources or arrange fieldwork inside North Korea, and does not engage with North Korean persons or entities. Its work is limited to lawful research from outside the country into whether a client's own counterparties, contractors or transactions may have a concealed North Korean connection.

      How can a company tell if a remote developer may be a North Korean IT worker?

      The May 2022 advisory from the State Department, Treasury and FBI lists indicators, including logins from many IP addresses, inconsistent names or work histories, requests to ship equipment to another address, and payments routed to third-country accounts. No single indicator is conclusive; TDSC helps document a pattern so counsel can decide on next steps.

      Where does reliable information on North Korean networks come from?

      Primarily from US government designations and advisories, United Nations Security Council materials including Panel of Experts reports published before its mandate lapsed in 2024, enforcement and court records in third countries, ship registries and trade data, and specialist research. Corporate records in jurisdictions where front companies are incorporated are often the most useful link.

      What should a business do if it finds a possible North Korean link?

      It should involve sanctions counsel promptly before taking further steps, preserve relevant records and avoid tipping off or further dealing with the counterparty pending advice. TDSC can provide a sourced factual report to support that review but does not advise on legal obligations, licensing or disclosure decisions.

      Does TDSC accept engagements from clients connected to North Korea?

      No engagement is accepted that would involve providing services to, or for the benefit of, the North Korean government or persons subject to sanctions, or that would otherwise breach US or other applicable sanctions. Every matter is screened before acceptance and may be declined without detailed explanation.
