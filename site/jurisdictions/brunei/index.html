      Overview

      Brunei at a Glance

      Brunei Darussalam is a small, high-income sultanate on the island of Borneo with a legal system built on English common law, operating alongside Syariah law in defined areas. Its commercial environment is compact and relationship-driven: a limited number of conglomerates, government-linked companies and foreign joint-venture partners account for much of the formal economy. Corporate records are administered by the Registry of Companies and Business Names within the Ministry of Finance and Economy, and a comprehensive private-sector data protection law, the Personal Data Protection Order 2025, now governs how personal data is collected and used. Investigative work in this setting depends on careful sourcing, discretion and an understanding of how few degrees of separation typically exist between parties.

      Organisations commission investigative work involving Brunei most often in connection with the energy sector, where upstream oil and gas, liquefied natural gas and downstream refining and petrochemical projects attract foreign contractors, suppliers and financiers. Other drivers include vetting of local agents and joint-venture partners required for market entry, supplier and distributor checks in construction and government procurement, and asset or background research in disputes that cross into Malaysia, Singapore or Indonesia. Investors in Islamic finance and halal-sector ventures also seek reputational and ownership verification. Typical exposures include undisclosed related-party interests, concentration of influence in a small business community, and reliance on intermediaries whose standing is difficult to verify from outside the country.

      Law & Regulation

      What applies before any work touching Brunei begins

      Legal system

      Brunei has a mixed system: English common law heritage applies in civil and commercial matters, while Syariah courts exercise jurisdiction in defined areas of personal and religious law. Commercial disputes are heard by the Subordinate and Intermediate Courts within limited jurisdiction, and by the High Court, which has unlimited original jurisdiction. Appeals lie to the Court of Appeal, which includes international judges, with a limited further civil appeal to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council. Arbitration is governed by the Arbitration Order 2009 and the International Arbitration Order 2009, and the government-established Brunei Darussalam Arbitration Centre (2014) administers cases.

      Data protection

      Personal Data Protection Order, 2025 (PDPO) · Regulator: Authority for Info-communications Technology Industry of Brunei Darussalam (AITI)

      The PDPO, enacted in January 2025 with most substantive provisions taking effect in 2026 after a phased implementation period, governs private-sector collection, use and disclosure of personal data, including employee and customer data. Organisations must limit collection to what is reasonable for the purpose, rely on consent or a recognised exception, protect and dispose of data appropriately, and appoint a data protection officer. Transfers outside Brunei must receive protection comparable to the PDPO; AITI points to the ASEAN Model Contractual Clauses as a voluntary template. Notifiable breaches must be reported to AITI within three days of assessment. Investigative work involving individuals therefore needs a documented purpose and lawful basis before any personal data is gathered or exported.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified a dedicated statute or publicly documented licensing regime for private investigators or detective agencies in Brunei, and we treat the position as unconfirmed. Activities adjacent to investigation remain constrained by general law: the PDPO limits collection and disclosure of personal data by private organisations, and criminal law addresses trespass, harassment and unauthorised access to computer systems. Records held by government agencies are generally not open to private inquiry. For any field work, surveillance or interview programme, TDSC confirms the current position with Brunei counsel and works only through appropriately qualified local professionals.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies and business names are registered with the Registry of Companies and Business Names (ROCBN), part of the Ministry of Finance and Economy, under the Companies Act (Chapter 39) and the Business Names Act (Chapter 92). Registration and filings are handled through the Online Company Profile (OCP) portal, and the registry provides access to documents held on its register, such as company profiles, officers and filed returns, subject to its procedures and fees. Since 2020 companies have been required to maintain and file information on registrable controllers; we have not confirmed that this beneficial ownership information is publicly accessible, and it should be treated as restricted. Court and land records are not well documented for remote access.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery is addressed principally by the Prevention of Corruption Act (Chapter 131), enforced by the Anti-Corruption Bureau, which also investigates related money laundering. The Criminal Asset Recovery Order, 2012 (CARO) provides the main anti-money laundering framework, including confiscation and suspicious transaction reporting, alongside the Anti-Terrorism Order, 2011. Reports are made to the Financial Intelligence Unit of the Brunei Darussalam Central Bank. The Royal Brunei Police Force and the Attorney General's Chambers also play central roles in enforcement and mutual legal assistance.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Brunei

      TDSC handles matters touching Brunei from Nashville through a scoped consulting engagement. Each matter begins with a jurisdictional review of what may lawfully be gathered, taking account of the PDPO, the small size of the local business community and the reputational sensitivity that follows from it. We prioritise documentary sources: ROCBN filings, published judgments, regional registries in Malaysia and Singapore, and reputable media. Where inquiries on the ground are justified, we coordinate qualified local professionals and confirm the approach with Brunei counsel in advance, keeping a low profile throughout. Personal data is minimised, transferred only on a documented basis consistent with the PDPO transfer rules, and held securely in the United States for the duration agreed with the client.

      TDSC has no office in Brunei. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Brunei: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can company ownership in Brunei be verified from abroad?

      Basic company information and filed documents can generally be obtained from the Registry of Companies and Business Names through its procedures and the OCP portal. Controller information that companies file is not, as far as we have confirmed, publicly available, so ultimate ownership is often established by combining registry documents, regional filings in Malaysia or Singapore and other lawful sources.

      How does the Personal Data Protection Order affect a background inquiry?

      Since the PDPO took effect, private organisations need a reasonable purpose and consent or a recognised exception to collect personal data, and must protect any data they transfer abroad. We therefore define the purpose of each inquiry in writing, limit data collected to what that purpose requires, and route any locally gathered information through a documented transfer basis.

      Do private investigators need a licence in Brunei?

      We have not identified a dedicated licensing regime for private investigators in Brunei, and we do not treat that absence as permission. Field activity remains subject to criminal law, data protection and local sensitivities. Before any work on the ground, we confirm the current position with Brunei counsel and use only appropriately qualified local professionals.

      Why is discretion especially important in Brunei matters?

      Brunei has a population of under half a million and a concentrated business community in which counterparties, officials and advisers are often connected. Inquiries that are poorly framed can quickly become known to the subject. We favour documentary research first and limit direct approaches to those agreed with the client and counsel.

      Can TDSC help with disputes involving Brunei parties seated elsewhere?

      Yes. Many disputes with Brunei parties are litigated or arbitrated in Singapore, London or under the International Arbitration Order in Brunei. We support counsel with lawful background research, asset tracing through public records and witness identification, while leaving legal strategy and any Brunei law advice to qualified local lawyers.
