      Republic of Haiti

      Haiti: Investigative Consulting

      Investigative work involving Haiti is shaped by a French-derived civil law system, records that are often paper-based, a thin data protection framework and a severe security environment. TDSC scopes these matters from Nashville, relying on lawful documentary sources and carefully vetted local professionals.

      Overview

      Haiti at a Glance

      Haiti is a civil law jurisdiction whose codes date to the 1820s, with French and Haitian Creole as the constitutional languages. Laws take effect on publication in Le Moniteur, the official gazette. Commercial records are held by the Ministry of Commerce and Industry, which digitised its historic registers and opened an electronic registry search in 2012. Many land, court and civil status records remain paper-based and dispersed. Since March 2024 the country has been under a national state of emergency, and armed groups control much of Port-au-Prince. As of 2026 the US Department of State advises against all travel, which directly limits what can safely be done on the ground.

      Organisations commission work involving Haiti for practical reasons. Remittances, food and fuel imports, apparel assembly, telecommunications, construction and donor-funded programmes create counterparty and procurement exposure. US and Canadian diaspora links generate family, estate and property questions that surface in North American litigation. Lenders and humanitarian organisations need assurance about local partners, beneficial owners and politically exposed persons, particularly where US authorities have designated certain Haitian gangs as terrorist organisations and where kidnapping, extortion and the diversion of aid remain recurring risks.

      Typical Matters

      Where Investigative Work Involving Haiti Concentrates

      01

      Aid and procurement partner vetting

      Donor-funded programmes and NGOs rely on Haitian contractors, transporters and distributors. Vetting covers registration in the MCI records, ownership and family links, political exposure and adverse media, together with any reported links to armed groups that control routes, ports or distribution points.

      02

      Diaspora estate and property questions

      Haitian families in Florida, New York, Massachusetts and Quebec often face estate and land disputes with Haitian elements. Work focuses on locating civil status and notarial records, understanding how title is documented locally, and identifying what can be proven from documents without travel.

      03

      Remittance and payments counterparties

      Money transfer operators, banks and fintech providers serving the Haiti corridor need assurance about agents and local partners. Reviews examine corporate standing, principals, regulatory history with the central bank and exposure to extortion or sanctioned persons.

      04

      Import and distribution due diligence

      Fuel, food, construction materials and consumer goods move through a small number of importers and port operators. Enquiries map ownership, market position, litigation and political connections so that suppliers and lenders understand concentration and integrity risk before contracting.

      05

      Asset tracing for North American claims

      Creditors and litigants sometimes need to know whether a Haitian individual or company holds assets in the United States, Canada or the Dominican Republic. Tracing work relies on records in those jurisdictions alongside what Haitian public sources can lawfully show.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Haiti

      Legal system

      Haiti follows the French civil law tradition. Its Civil, Civil Procedure, Commercial, Criminal and Criminal Procedure codes were enacted in 1825-1826 and have been amended since. The Cour de Cassation sits at the apex, above five regional courts of appeal and sixteen tribunaux de premiere instance, which hear civil, commercial and criminal matters at first instance; justices of the peace handle smaller claims. There is no separate commercial court. New penal and criminal procedure codes were ratified in 2025, but their entry into force was postponed in December 2025 in favour of phased implementation.

      Data protection

      Arrete on the protection of personal data (published in Le Moniteur, 15 May 2018); Penal Code provisions on data offences · Regulator: No dedicated data protection authority has been established

      Haiti has no comprehensive data protection statute. A 2018 ministerial arrete prohibits disclosure of personal data that could infringe individuals' rights and freedoms, requires data to be stored securely with access limited to authorised persons, and gives data subjects the ability to correct data transferred to third parties. There is no regulator, registration requirement or mandatory breach notification. The criminal code framework addresses unauthorised access to computer systems and interception of correspondence. In practice, investigative work should minimise personal data, avoid any interception or covert recording, and handle records transferred out of Haiti under the client's own data protection obligations.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified a dedicated licensing regime for private investigators or detective agencies in Haiti. Private security companies are governed by decrees from 1988 and 1989, which commentators have described as lacking specificity and dividing oversight among several bodies. Any local professional engaged for field enquiries should therefore be assessed individually, and engagements are scoped with Haitian counsel. Activities such as interception of communications or unauthorised access to computer systems are criminal offences, and security conditions in Port-au-Prince and parts of the Artibonite currently make most in-person enquiries impractical.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies are registered with the Ministry of Commerce and Industry (MCI). Its Electronic Registry of Commerce, opened in 2012, digitised more than 30,000 records covering corporations, partnerships, trade names and intellectual property, but the public can see only limited information, chiefly whether a name is registered. Constitutive documents of societes anonymes are published in Le Moniteur, which can be a useful source for founders and early officers. We have not identified a public beneficial ownership register. Land title and court records are generally held locally on paper and are often incomplete.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      The Law on the Prevention and Suppression of Corruption, promulgated in 2014, criminalises bribery, illicit enrichment, embezzlement of public property, influence peddling, nepotism and conflicts of interest in public contracting. The Unite de Lutte Contre la Corruption (ULCC) leads prevention and investigation. The law of 11 November 2013 on money laundering and terrorist financing, amended in 2016, is the main AML statute. The Unite Centrale de Renseignements Financiers (UCREF) is the financial intelligence unit.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Haiti

      TDSC handles Haiti matters from Nashville and does not send staff into the country while current security conditions persist. Each engagement begins with a jurisdictional review of what is needed, what Haitian law permits and what can realistically be obtained from MCI records, Le Moniteur, court filings and open sources, and from records in the United States, Canada and the Dominican Republic. Every matter is screened against applicable sanctions and terrorism designations, including those covering Haitian armed groups. Where documents must be retrieved locally, we coordinate appropriately qualified professionals and Haitian counsel, with attention to their safety. Personal data is collected only as far as the purpose requires and is held under the client's data protection framework.

      TDSC has no office in Haiti. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Haiti: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can company ownership in Haiti be verified from public records?

      Partly. The Ministry of Commerce and Industry registry confirms registration and names, and constitutive acts of societes anonymes appear in Le Moniteur, which often identifies founding shareholders. We have not identified a public beneficial ownership register, so current ownership usually has to be corroborated through filings, counterpart documents, media and enquiries made with local counsel.

      Does Haiti license private investigators?

      We have not identified a dedicated licensing regime for private investigators. Private security companies operate under decrees from the late 1980s. Because there is no clear professional framework, TDSC assesses any Haitian professional individually, defines their tasks narrowly in writing and keeps activities within what Haitian counsel confirms is lawful.

      Is on-the-ground work possible given the security situation?

      Only to a limited extent. As of 2026 the US Department of State advises against all travel, armed groups control much of the capital and US commercial flights to Port-au-Prince are suspended. We prioritise documentary and remote sources and commission local tasks only where the information justifies it and the risk to the people involved is acceptable.

      How are land and property questions handled?

      Haitian title is frequently documented through notarial acts and surveys held locally, and records may be incomplete or contested. We identify which documents exist, who holds them and how they could be obtained or authenticated, and we work with Haitian counsel on what the documents actually prove. We do not offer opinions on title ourselves.

      What about sanctions and terrorism risk?

      US authorities have designated certain Haitian gangs as terrorist organisations, and several Haitian individuals are subject to sanctions. Counterparty work therefore includes screening for direct and indirect links to designated persons. TDSC does not accept engagements that would involve dealings prohibited by US or other applicable sanctions.
