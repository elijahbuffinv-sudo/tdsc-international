      Overview

      Puerto Rico at a Glance

      Puerto Rico is an unincorporated territory of the United States, so federal statutes, federal courts and federal regulators apply alongside the Commonwealth's own legislature, courts and agencies. Private law follows the civil law tradition inherited from Spain and was recodified in the Civil Code of 2020, while procedure and public law draw heavily on United States models. Commonwealth trials are conducted in Spanish and much of the primary documentation an investigator needs, from corporate filings to judgments, is in Spanish. Investigations therefore have to move between two legal vocabularies and two court systems, and between records held by the Department of State, the Commonwealth judiciary and the US District Court for the District of Puerto Rico.

      Organisations commission work involving Puerto Rico for reasons tied to its particular economy. The island hosts a long-established pharmaceutical and medical device manufacturing base, a financial and insurance sector, and a growing population of individuals and companies relocating under the tax incentives consolidated in the Puerto Rico Incentives Code (Act 60-2019). Public contracting, disaster recovery funding and the fiscal oversight process established under the federal PROMESA statute have also drawn scrutiny of contractors and counterparties. Typical exposures include misrepresented residency or business presence, related-party contracting, asset concealment in litigation, employee misconduct in regulated facilities, and reputational questions about counterparties in public procurement.

      Law & Regulation

      What applies before any work touching Puerto Rico begins

      Legal system

      Puerto Rico has a mixed legal system: private law (obligations, contracts, property, family, succession) follows the civil law tradition and is codified in the Civil Code of 2020 (Act 55-2020), while constitutional, procedural and much public law follows United States common law models. Commonwealth courts comprise the Court of First Instance, the Court of Appeals and the Supreme Court of Puerto Rico. Federal matters go to the US District Court for the District of Puerto Rico and the First Circuit. Arbitration is governed, as of 2025, by the Puerto Rico Arbitration Act (Act 147-2024).

      Data protection

      No comprehensive data protection statute; sectoral laws including the Citizen Information on Data Banks Security Act (Act 111-2005), plus applicable US federal law · Regulator: Department of Consumer Affairs (DACO) for Act 111 breach reporting; federal regulators for sector-specific rules

      Puerto Rico has no single comprehensive privacy law as of 2025. Act 111-2005 requires notice to affected individuals and a report to DACO when certain unencrypted personal information is breached, and other statutes govern social security numbers, data disposal and specific categories of information. Federal regimes such as the Fair Credit Reporting Act apply to background screening. The Puerto Rico Constitution protects privacy, and that right can be enforced directly against private parties, including employers, without state action. Case law generally expects prior notice before electronic monitoring of employees. Cross-border transfers are not specifically regulated by the local data laws.

      Private investigators

      Private detectives and detective agencies in Puerto Rico are licensed under Act No. 108 of 29 June 1965, as amended, and Regulation No. 9262. Licensing is administered by the Puerto Rico Police Bureau through its private security licensing division. Applicants must, among other requirements, be US citizens, complete at least 1,000 hours of study at an authorised private detective school, pass a written examination, post a bond or insurance, provide fingerprints and hold no public office. Field investigation in Puerto Rico therefore requires a locally licensed detective. Recording, surveillance and access to records remain subject to constitutional privacy protections and Commonwealth and federal law.

      Corporate & public records

      Corporations, limited liability companies and other entities are registered with the Puerto Rico Department of State through its online Registry of Corporations and Legal Entities. Articles of incorporation identify the resident agent, designated office, authorised stock and incorporator, and entities file annual reports with the Department of State, with a deadline of 15 April for corporations. Share ownership is not recorded in a public register. Following FinCEN's March 2025 interim final rule, entities created in Puerto Rico are treated as domestic US companies and are exempt from federal beneficial ownership reporting. Commonwealth and federal court records are available through the respective judiciaries.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      The Anti-Corruption Code for the New Puerto Rico (Act 2-2018) consolidates local anti-corruption provisions, bars persons and companies convicted of specified offences from contracting with executive agencies and requires bidders to file sworn declarations on prior convictions. The Department of Justice, the Office of Government Ethics, the Office of the Comptroller and the Office of the Special Independent Prosecutor Panel all have roles. US federal anti-corruption and anti-money laundering statutes, including the Bank Secrecy Act, also apply.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Puerto Rico

      TDSC scopes Puerto Rico engagements from Nashville and begins by identifying which law governs each task, since Commonwealth privacy and licensing rules sit alongside federal statutes such as the Fair Credit Reporting Act. Desk research draws on the Department of State registry, Commonwealth and federal court records, property and regulatory sources and Spanish-language media, reviewed by Spanish-proficient analysts. Where fieldwork, interviews or surveillance are needed, TDSC coordinates detectives licensed under Act 108-1965 and works with Puerto Rico counsel on privilege, employee monitoring and the admissibility of evidence. Personal data is collected only for a defined purpose, held in access-controlled systems and returned or destroyed under the engagement terms. TDSC does not hold a Puerto Rico licence and does not provide legal advice.

      TDSC has no office in Puerto Rico. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Puerto Rico: Frequently Asked Questions

      Does a private investigator need a Puerto Rico licence even though it is a US territory?

      Yes. Puerto Rico licenses its own private detectives under Act 108-1965, administered by the Puerto Rico Police Bureau. A licence from a US state does not authorise investigative work on the island. TDSC therefore conducts research from Nashville and engages locally licensed detectives for any fieldwork, interviews or surveillance carried out in Puerto Rico.

      Can you verify whether someone genuinely relocated to Puerto Rico under Act 60?

      We can research the publicly observable footprint of a claimed relocation: entity filings, property records, litigation, professional activity and media coverage. We do not access tax records, decree files or government systems that are not public. Findings help clients and counsel assess consistency, while any formal residency question remains a matter for tax advisers and the relevant authorities.

      Are Puerto Rico court records in English?

      Federal court records from the US District Court for the District of Puerto Rico are in English. Commonwealth court proceedings and many filings are in Spanish, as are most Department of State documents. TDSC reviews Spanish-language material directly and can arrange certified translation when documents are needed for proceedings outside Puerto Rico.

      Can an employer in Puerto Rico monitor employees during an internal investigation?

      Puerto Rico recognises a constitutional right to privacy that applies to private employers, and case law generally expects employees to receive prior notice of electronic monitoring. Any monitoring, device review or covert observation should be planned with Puerto Rico employment counsel before it starts. TDSC structures investigative steps around that advice.

      Is beneficial ownership information for Puerto Rico companies publicly available?

      No public beneficial ownership register exists. Since FinCEN's March 2025 interim final rule, Puerto Rico entities are treated as domestic and are exempt from federal beneficial ownership reporting. Ownership is therefore reconstructed from filings, officers and agents, related entities, litigation, property records and other lawful sources, with conclusions stated at the confidence the evidence supports.
