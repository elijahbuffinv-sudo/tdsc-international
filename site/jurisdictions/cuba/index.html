      Overview

      Cuba at a Glance

      Cuba has a state-directed economy in which state enterprises, many linked to the military holding company GAESA, dominate tourism, retail, remittances and foreign trade, alongside a small but growing sector of private micro, small and medium enterprises and cooperatives. Information is scarce and controlled: there is no tradition of open corporate registries, the press is state-run, and personal data is governed by constitutional and regulatory provisions administered by state bodies. For a US firm, the decisive framework is external: the Cuban Assets Control Regulations and, since September 2026, the Cuba Sanctions Regulations, which restrict almost all dealings by persons subject to US jurisdiction.

      Organisations outside Cuba commission work touching the country mainly to manage exposure. European, Canadian and Latin American companies in hotels, mining, tobacco, rum and shipping need to understand whether partners, suppliers or vessels connect to sanctioned or restricted Cuban entities. Banks and payment firms screen counterparties for links to the Cuba Restricted List and to persons designated under Executive Order 14404. Claimants with certified property claims and litigants assessing Title III exposure under the Libertad Act also require careful background research, conducted strictly from lawful sources outside Cuba.

      Legal Environment

      How the law shapes investigative work involving Cuba

      Legal system

      Cuba has a civil law system rooted in Spanish codification and shaped by socialist legal principles, under the Constitution adopted in 2019. Courts are organised in a hierarchy of municipal and provincial people's courts with the People's Supreme Court at the apex, and economic disputes involving enterprises are heard in specialised economic chambers. International commercial disputes involving foreign investors are commonly referred to the Court of International Commercial Arbitration attached to the Chamber of Commerce of the Republic of Cuba. The independence of the judiciary from the state and party is limited.

      Data protection

      Constitution of the Republic of Cuba (2019), Article 97, and Decree-Law 35/2021 on telecommunications and information and communication technologies, with associated regulations · Regulator: Ministry of Communications (MINCOM), in coordination with the Ministry of the Interior and other authorities

      According to DLA Piper's guide, last reviewed in 2022, Cuba does not have a standalone comprehensive data protection statute of the GDPR type; privacy is protected through Article 97 of the 2019 Constitution and Decree-Law 35/2021, enforced by the Ministry of Communications in coordination with the Ministry of the Interior and the Central Bank. In practice, personal data is closely held by the state and there is no lawful commercial market in Cuban personal records. Investigative work for TDSC is therefore confined to open and published sources accessible outside Cuba, with any personal data handled under the client's own home-jurisdiction rules.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified any legal framework permitting private investigation or detective agencies to operate in Cuba, and the economic and security context makes independent fact-finding inside the country impractical and potentially unlawful. Information gathering on the island is the domain of state bodies. TDSC does not conduct or commission field enquiries, interviews or record retrieval in Cuba. Work is limited to desk research from lawful sources outside the country, such as sanctions lists, international corporate registries, court records in other jurisdictions, maritime data and published reporting.

      Corporate & public records

      Cuba does not offer a public, searchable online company registry comparable to European or American registries, and ownership of state enterprises is not transparent. Many enterprises in tourism, retail and finance are reported to sit within the GAESA group controlled by the Revolutionary Armed Forces, and the US State Department publishes the Cuba Restricted List of entities and subentities with which direct financial transactions are prohibited for persons subject to US jurisdiction. Foreign joint venture partners and their holding companies can often be researched in their home registries, which is where most verifiable ownership information is found.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Corruption, bribery and embezzlement are offences under the Cuban Penal Code, and the Comptroller General of the Republic oversees the state economic sector; prosecution rests with the Attorney General's Office. For investigators and clients outside Cuba, the operative compliance frameworks are usually their own: the US Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, the UK Bribery Act or equivalent laws, together with the sanctions regulations that govern any Cuba-related activity.

      Engagement Standard

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Cuba

      TDSC, as a US company, screens every matter touching Cuba for sanctions compliance before accepting it and does not accept engagements that would violate US or other applicable sanctions. The Cuban Assets Control Regulations and the Cuba Sanctions Regulations, both amended or issued with effect from 30 September 2026, prohibit most dealings by persons subject to US jurisdiction, so engagements are generally limited to lawful due diligence and risk advisory for clients outside Cuba. TDSC does not travel to Cuba for engagements, engage persons in Cuba, pay Cuban entities or obtain records from Cuban sources. Research relies on sanctions lists, foreign registries, court records and published reporting. Questions about licences or exemptions are referred to the client's sanctions counsel.

      TDSC has no office in Cuba. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Cuba: Frequently Asked Questions

      Can TDSC carry out investigations inside Cuba?

      No. As a US company, TDSC is subject to the Cuban Assets Control Regulations and the Cuba Sanctions Regulations, and we have identified no lawful framework for private investigation in Cuba. TDSC does not conduct field work, engage local sources or obtain records in Cuba. Work is confined to sanctions-screened desk research from lawful sources outside the country.

      What changed in US Cuba sanctions in 2026?

      Executive Order 14404, issued on 1 May 2026, created new authorities targeting those responsible for repression in Cuba. OFAC issued the Cuba Sanctions Regulations at 31 CFR Part 516 and amended the CACR, both effective 30 September 2026, tightening travel categories, Cuba Restricted List transactions and U-turn payments. Specific questions should go to sanctions counsel.

      Can non-US companies be affected by these sanctions?

      Yes. OFAC has stated that non-US persons involved in specified Cuba-related activities face sanctions risk under Executive Order 14404, and that foreign financial institutions conducting or facilitating significant transactions for persons blocked under it may themselves be sanctioned. This is one reason non-US clients commission screening of Cuban counterparties and intermediaries.

      How can ownership of a Cuban enterprise be established?

      Usually only partially. Cuba has no open corporate registry, and many enterprises are state-owned or reported to sit within military-linked groups such as GAESA. Research relies on the Cuba Restricted List, OFAC designations, foreign joint venture partners' home-country filings, court records abroad and credible published reporting, with conclusions expressed as assessed confidence rather than certainty.

      Does Cuba have a data protection law?

      DLA Piper's guide, last reviewed in 2022, describes no standalone comprehensive statute and points to Article 97 of the 2019 Constitution and Decree-Law 35/2021, overseen by the Ministry of Communications. Because TDSC does not obtain data from Cuban sources, personal data in its work is handled under the client's home-jurisdiction data protection rules.
