      Overview

      Grenada at a Glance

      Grenada is a small English-speaking parliamentary democracy in the Eastern Caribbean, with a common-law legal system and courts that form part of the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court, with final appeal to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council. It shares the Eastern Caribbean dollar and a central bank with other OECS members. Public records are more limited and less digitised than in larger jurisdictions, and as of 2024 the US government reported that legislation specific to data protection had not reached the national legislative agenda. Investigative work therefore relies on a careful combination of registry and court searches conducted locally, published regional sources and discreet, well-informed inquiry in a community where professional and family networks overlap closely.

      Organisations commission work touching Grenada mainly in connection with tourism and resort development, real estate, its investment migration programme, the offshore medical education sector and regional trade. Typical needs include verifying developers and partners in hotel and residential projects, background research on individuals connected to investment-linked citizenship or projects marketed to foreign buyers, checking counterparties in agriculture and nutmeg and cocoa exports, and supporting litigation or enforcement before the High Court. Foreign investors, lenders and law firms also seek to understand the reputation and track record of Grenadian principals and the politically exposed connections that can arise in a small jurisdiction.

      Jurisdictional Framework

      The rules that govern lawful inquiry in Grenada

      Legal system

      Grenada has a common-law system derived from English law. Its courts comprise the Magistrates' Courts and the High Court of Justice, which together with the Court of Appeal form part of the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court, headquartered in Castries, Saint Lucia. The Court of Appeal travels among member states. Final appeals lie to the Judicial Committee of the Privy Council in London. The Caribbean Court of Justice has original jurisdiction over disputes on the Revised Treaty of Chaguaramas. Arbitration and court-connected mediation are available for commercial disputes.

      Data protection

      No comprehensive data protection statute identified as of 2024 · Regulator: None identified

      According to the US State Department's 2024 Investment Climate Statement, laws specific to data storage and protection had not yet reached Grenada's national legislative agenda, and OECS stakeholders were developing model laws. In the absence of a comprehensive statute, investigative work involving personal data is governed by general law, including confidentiality obligations, banking secrecy, defamation and the constitutional protection of privacy, and by the data protection law of the client's own jurisdiction, such as the GDPR or US state laws. TDSC applies data minimisation and purpose limitation as a matter of practice and confirms the current position with Grenadian counsel.

      Private investigators

      We have not identified, from sources verified for this page, a dedicated licensing regime for private investigators in Grenada. Private security activity may be subject to regulation, and the position for investigative services should be confirmed with Grenadian counsel before field work begins. Private investigators have no special powers: police, immigration, tax and banking records are not accessible to private parties, and access to court files may require an application. In a small jurisdiction, discretion is especially important, and TDSC coordinates only local professionals whose standing and methods have been checked.

      Corporate & public records

      Companies are incorporated under the Companies Act, as amended in 2011, and records are kept by the Corporate Affairs and Intellectual Property Office (CAIPO). Searches of company files, including directors and registered office details, are generally conducted in person or through local agents rather than through a comprehensive public online database. We have not verified whether Grenada operates a central register of beneficial owners or its access rules. Land records are held through the Deeds and Land Registry, and searches are normally conducted by local counsel. High Court filings may be searched at the Registry in St. George's.

      Anti-corruption & AML

      Bribery is a criminal offence in Grenada. The Integrity in Public Life Act (Act No. 24 of 2013) requires public officials to declare income and assets to the Integrity Commission, which monitors and verifies those declarations. Grenada is party to the Inter-American Convention against Corruption. Suspicious financial activity is reported to the Financial Intelligence Unit, and AML obligations are set by proceeds-of-crime legislation and associated regulations. We have not verified the specific titles and years of the principal anti-bribery and AML statutes for this page.

      How TDSC Scopes Matters Touching Grenada

      TDSC scopes Grenadian matters with particular attention to discretion, since inquiries in a small community can quickly become known. Each engagement starts with a review of the purpose, the parties, any political exposure and the legal context, including which data protection law governs the client and how findings may be used before the Eastern Caribbean courts or elsewhere. Desk research draws on regional court decisions, Caribbean press, international corporate and litigation records and the subjects' links to other jurisdictions. Where company, land or court searches must be made in St. George's, TDSC coordinates qualified local professionals and works alongside the client's Grenadian counsel, who advises on legal questions. TDSC does not seek immigration, police or banking records.

      TDSC has no office in Grenada. Engagements are directed from Nashville, Tennessee; where work on the ground is needed, it is performed by appropriately licensed local professionals under a written scope. TDSC does not provide legal advice.

      Questions

      Grenada: Frequently Asked Questions

      Does Grenada have a data protection law?

      As of the US State Department's 2024 Investment Climate Statement, laws specific to data protection had not reached Grenada's legislative agenda. That does not leave personal data unprotected: confidentiality, privacy and banking secrecy rules apply, and clients' own laws often govern. We confirm the current position with Grenadian counsel at the start of each matter.

      Can company records in Grenada be searched online?

      Only to a limited extent. Company files are held by the Corporate Affairs and Intellectual Property Office, and fuller searches are commonly made through local agents or counsel. We combine these searches with international corporate records, since many Grenadian-connected businesses also have entities in other Caribbean or foreign jurisdictions.

      Where are commercial disputes involving Grenada heard?

      In the High Court of Justice in St. George's, part of the Eastern Caribbean Supreme Court. Appeals go to the Eastern Caribbean Court of Appeal and finally to the Privy Council in London. Common-law procedure and English-language proceedings make it familiar territory for US and UK counsel.

      Can TDSC verify an investor in a Grenadian development project?

      Yes, through lawful sources. We research the investor's corporate and litigation history, media coverage, sanctions and regulatory exposure and the plausibility of declared wealth, working mainly in the jurisdictions where the investor actually lives and does business. We do not access government programme files or immigration records.

      Are there particular risks in Caribbean resort projects?

      Recurring issues include developers with limited completion history, pre-sale funds not held as represented, overlapping ownership of land or entities across islands and disputes with contractors. Independent verification of sponsors, their prior projects and their regional litigation record before committing funds helps clients avoid the most common problems.
